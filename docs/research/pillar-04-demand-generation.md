## 1. Pillars — load-bearing principles

**P4.1 — Free is not a low price; it is a different category of price.**
Shampanier, Mazar & Ariely (2007, *Marketing Science*) show demand behaves discontinuously at zero: shifting a Hershey's Kiss from 1¢ to free flipped choice shares from 73/27 in favour of the premium Lindt truffle to 31/69 in favour of the free item, holding the price *difference* constant. The authors' preferred mechanism is **affect**, not cost-benefit reasoning. `[PRIMARY]` Consequence for this pillar: nothing that reasons about "a very cheap first engagement" transfers to a free one, and vice versa. This is why the paid-pilot literature and the free-work literature must be read as two literatures.

**P4.2 — For *services*, free and exaggerated prices both depress perceived quality.**
Gorn, Tse & Weinberg (1991, *Marketing Letters* 2:99–110) is the single most on-point primary source in this pillar: *"when the tested services are offered for free and for exaggerated prices, they are perceived as lower in quality than when they are offered in a normal price range."* `[PRIMARY]` This is P1's C10 measured — on services, not goods — and it says the feared direction is real. It also, incidentally, corroborates P1's C8 from the other end: the *top* of the range carries a quality penalty too.

**P4.3 — Reference prices form from observed past prices, and the first observed price shapes the acceptable range.**
Kalyanaram & Winer's (1995) empirical generalisations: consumers demonstrably use reference prices, and **past prices are part of reference-price formation**. Kalyanaram & Little (1994) find a **region of price insensitivity (latitude of acceptance) around the reference price**, wider for higher average reference prices. `[PRIMARY]` Sitzia & Zizzo (2012, *J. Econ. Psychology* 33(6):1084–1099) tested the ordering directly and found **high-then-low more profitable than low-then-high**, explained by preference shaping: observing low prices produces a low reference price and a belief that a *bad deal* is being made when price rises. `[PRIMARY]`

**P4.4 — Promotion depth repeats into price sensitivity; it is not a one-off cost.**
Mela, Gupta & Lehmann (1997, *JMR* 34(2):248–261), 8¼ years of panel data: **promotions increase consumer price sensitivity and decrease brand equity over time**; reduced advertising plus increased promotion makes consumers more price- and promotion-sensitive. `[PRIMARY]` The sponsored build is the deepest possible promotion. P4.4 says the cost is not confined to the sponsored engagement.

**P4.5 — Offer design, not messaging, is the lever on the dominant loss mechanism.**
Inherited from P2 and not weakened by anything found here: 40–60% of qualified B2B deals end in no decision; of those, **56% to indecision/FOMU, 44% to status-quo preference** (Dixon & McKenna, 2.5M recorded conversations). `[PRACTITIONER-AUTHORITATIVE, large-N]` A campaign that is entirely persuasion addresses the minority mechanism. What addresses FOMU is a *structure*: a bounded first commitment, a bond, an acceptance gate.

**P4.6 — There is no evidenced temporal hierarchy of advertising effects.**
Vakratsas & Ambler (1999, *Journal of Marketing* 63(1)), reviewing 250+ articles and books: **"little support for any hierarchy, in the sense of temporal sequence, of effects."** `[PRIMARY, review]` AIDA and its descendants are mnemonics with contested authorship (Iwamoto 2023 disputes the standard St. Elmo Lewis attribution, crediting Dukesmith and Sheldon). A 7-day plan whose logic is "Day 2 creates problem awareness so that Day 6 can convert" is asserting exactly the sequence this literature declines to support.

**P4.7 — Most of the addressable market is not buying now, and the purpose of reach is future recall.**
Ehrenberg-Bass / Dawes (LinkedIn B2B Institute): roughly **5% of B2B buyers are in-market in a given quarter, ~20% in a given year**, derived from an assumed ~5-year supplier replacement cycle. `[PRACTITIONER-INSTITUTE; and note the derivation is arithmetic from a cycle-length assumption, not a direct measurement of in-market status — the Institute's own page does not state the methodology]` A seven-day campaign optimised for immediate applications is, on this reading, harvesting the 5% and discarding the 95%.

**P4.8 — What buyers say builds trust is research and data, and most published thought leadership fails that test.**
Edelman–LinkedIn B2B Thought Leadership (n ≈ 3,500 decision-makers, 2024 wave): **55%** cite "strong research and data" as a top characteristic of quality; **fewer than 50%** rate the quality of what they read as good and only **15%** as very good; **75%** say thought leadership led them to research something they were not previously considering; **90%** are more receptive to outreach from consistent high-quality publishers; **70%** of C-suite say it has at least occasionally made them question an existing supplier; **86%** would be moderately/very likely to invite such an organisation into an RFP; **60%** say they would pay a premium. `[PRACTITIONER survey, large-N, vendor-interested — Edelman and LinkedIn both sell the implication]` This is the closest thing found to *evidence* for P3's Spence-derived, theory-only claim that falsifiable published artefacts are the available substitute at zero client history.

**P4.9 — Skin in the game is the practitioner consensus for early-customer programs, and the strongest documented case charged.**
Sierra's design-partner program (First Round Review): a 40-name list screened to a target of 4 (landed 6); partners paid **10–20% of total contract value**; engagements run **2–6 months**; treated as *"paid enterprise sales contracts not early-stage pilots"*; deliberately **not** sourced primarily through warm introductions because *"strangers hold you to higher standards than friends"*; **100% of design partners converted to customers**. `[PRACTITIONER-AUTHORITATIVE, single case, survivorship-biased]`

**P4.10 — Scarcity works, and its strongest documented form is exactly the form the campaign uses — for exactly the product class Axon Enjin sells.**
Barton, Zlatevska & Oppewal (2022, *Journal of Retailing* 98(4):741–758), meta-analysis of **416 effect sizes from 131 studies**: scarcity cues generally raise purchase intention; **time-based scarcity is most effective for high-involvement products**; demand-based for utilitarian, supply-based for experiences. `[PRIMARY, meta-analysis]` A custom software build is high-involvement. Ladeira et al. (2023, *Psychology & Marketing*) find limited-quantity and limited-time **do not differ significantly** as urgency cues, and that *excessive demand* framing outperforms *restricted supply*. `[PRIMARY, meta-analysis]` Neither result licenses a false claim; both raise the value of a true one.

**P4.11 — The highest rung of lead generation is the slowest, and a firm at zero can only reach the lowest one immediately.**
Enns' Ladder of Lead Generation, three rungs: **Selling** (cold calls, email, trade shows, visits — a *private* claim of expertise, low power, high immediacy) → **Marketing** (advertising, sponsorship, direct mail, seminars — a *public declaration*, moderate validation, requires money) → **Educating** (books, speaking, branded research, podcasts — *third-party validation*, maximum power, long timelines). `[PRACTITIONER-AUTHORITATIVE, accessed via the 2Bobs episode and Win Without Pitching summaries; the primary page returned 403]` This is P3's C4.8/C3.8 allocation question restated as a ladder, and it says the same thing: the cash route and the time route are different rungs, and the good rung is slow.

**P4.12 — Verbal agreement is not behavioural compliance, and the classical compliance sequences differ sharply in how well they survive that distinction.**
Feeley, Anker & Aloe (2012, *Communication Monographs* 79(3):316–343) meta-analysed 35 years of door-in-the-face research: significant effect on **verbal** compliance (r ≈ .126, k = 78) but a **statistically insignificant** effect on **behavioural** compliance (r ≈ .052, k = 39). `[PRIMARY, meta-analysis — but see the source note in §9: the University at Buffalo press release attributes .126 to *behavioural* compliance, contradicting the figure surfaced from the abstract, and the abstract itself was inaccessible (403). The direction of the finding — verbal yes, behavioural no — is consistent across both accounts; the numeric assignment is not verified.]` Foot-in-the-door retains support (Freedman & Fraser 1966; Burger 1999 identifies self-perception, consistency, commitment and conformity as multiple mediating processes, i.e. the effect is real but heavily moderated).

## 2. Frameworks — named, multi-step, reusable

Continuing P3's numbering (P3 ended at F/31).

### F/32 — Schwartz's Two Ladders, kept separate `[ASSERTED — foundational practitioner (Breakthrough Advertising, 1966); no controlled test found]`

The brief asks for the distinction to be noted, and it must be, because practitioner sources routinely collapse them. One source consulted (motiveinmotion.com) printed the *sophistication* stages under the heading "levels of customer awareness." That conflation is the norm, not the exception.

**Ladder A — Awareness (a property of the individual prospect).** Most Aware → Product Aware → Solution Aware → Problem Aware → Completely Unaware. Determines *what the message must open with*: a price/CTA for the Most Aware; proof and differentiation for the Product Aware; comparison against alternatives for the Solution Aware; problem articulation for the Problem Aware; education and curiosity for the Unaware.

**Ladder B — Market sophistication (a property of the market's advertising history).** Stage 1: claims are new → make the simple direct claim and prove it. Stage 2: claims still work → enlarge and outbid. Stage 3: claims exhausted → introduce the **mechanism**. Stage 4: mechanisms competing → elaborate the mechanism. Stage 5: everything is worn out → shift from promise to **identification**.

**Why the distinction is load-bearing here.** The internal docs write to a Problem-Aware/Unaware prospect (Day 2, "Still using spreadsheets?") in a market that is at **Stage 3–4** on "automate your operations" — a claim space saturated by SaaS vendors, no-code platforms, and every other agency. Schwartz's own prescription for that combination is a *mechanism*, not a louder claim. P1's and P3's material supply candidate mechanisms (the multi-branch coordination thesis; the compliance-anchored category). No campaign asset in either internal doc leads with a mechanism.

### F/33 — The Unified Buying-Situation List (three literatures, one construct) `[SYNTHESIS — discharges P3's explicit handoff]`

See **§6.1** for the built list. The framework is the merge rule:

1. **Ehrenberg-Bass Category Entry Point (CEP)** — the memory cue that starts a buying situation. Purpose: *memory structure*. Question: "what were you doing/thinking when you decided you needed this?"
2. **Winning by Design Critical Event** — the dated internal occurrence that makes the status quo untenable. Purpose: *discovery slot / forecast gate*. Question: "what happened, and when?"
3. **Prospecting trigger signal (P2 F/16, F2.9)** — the same event, observed from outside via public data. Purpose: *target list*. Question: "who has one of these right now, and can I see it?"

**Merge rule:** an item belongs on the unified list if and only if it can be expressed in all three registers — as a cue a buyer would recall, as a dated internal event, and as an externally observable signal. Items that fail the third test are still CEPs and still discovery slots, but they are **not targetable**, and that distinction is the whole of P2's access problem.

### F/34 — Artefact-Backwards Engagement Design `[DERIVED — implements P3's handoff instruction directly]`

P3: *"A sponsored build that yields no citable figure has bought nothing."* The framework inverts normal scoping.

1. **Name the artefacts before naming the scope.** Target set, from P3 §6.2 items 1–4: (a) a reference call with a named person at a named organisation; (b) a warm introduction commitment; (c) a working demonstration runnable on the buyer's own data; (d) a case study containing at least one **checkable number**.
2. **Derive the measurement instrument, then the scope.** A checkable number requires a *pre-engagement baseline*. If hours-per-week on reconciliation is not measured before the build, no defensible after-number exists. **The baseline measurement is therefore the first deliverable, and it is the one the internal docs omit entirely.**
3. **Contract the artefacts, not the goodwill.** The 7-day plan's §Catch already lists testimonial consent and portfolio inclusion. F/34 adds: named-attribution consent, a named reference contact with a stated availability window, permission to publish the specific figure, and the baseline-measurement cooperation itself.
4. **Set an artefact acceptance gate.** Final scope items are released on artefact delivery, not on calendar date. (This is P3 §6.2's bond structure applied in reverse: the *client's* obligation is bonded.)
5. **Compute yield before committing** (F4.7). A build that can only ever produce items (c) and (d) is worth less than one that produces (a)–(d), and the difference is priceable.

### F/35 — The Free-to-Paid Ladder, with named decision points `[DERIVED — assembled from Enns' rungs, the paid-pilot/design-partner practitioner corpus, Gorn et al., and JOLT/FOMU]`

Seven rungs. The point of the framework is the **five gates**, not the rungs.

| Rung | Instrument | Price posture | What it buys |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | Published artefact (teardown with figures, micro-survey, named method) | Free, **non-rival** | Access + Spence-compliant credibility (P4.8) |
| 1 | Diagnostic conversation | Free, time-boxed, **capped and stated** | Qualification + switch-interview data |
| 2 | **Paid audit / paid discovery / roadmap** | Priced. Stark's published band for a strategy engagement is **~5% of overall project**; his own roadmapping product is listed at **USD 1,295** | A **positive** reference price, a bond, FOMU reduction |
| 3 | Bounded paid pilot | Priced at ≥ MVDS floor (P2 F2.1) | Delivery-reliability proof, artefacts (c)(d) |
| 4 | Sponsored/design-partner build | **The C10 exposure zone** (§6.9) | Artefacts (a)–(d) at the cost of a reference price |
| 5 | First full paid engagement | Full architecture (P1 F/3) | Margin |
| 6 | Retainer / expansion | P1 retainer benchmarks | LTV |

**Gate 1 — Rung 0 vs Rung 1 order.** Enns' ladder (P4.11) says Rung 0 is the high-power move and Rung 1 the low-power one, and that a firm at zero can only reach the low rung immediately. This is the cash-vs-time allocation question P3 left open (C3.8) and it is **not resolved here**.

**Gate 2 — Rung 2 vs Rung 4: the central decision of the entire campaign concept.** A paid audit and a sponsored build both produce artefacts and both de-risk the buyer. Only one of them sets a zero reference price. Gorn et al. (P4.2) and Sitzia & Zizzo (P4.3) both cut toward Rung 2. Sierra (P4.9) is a documented Rung-2/3 hybrid at 10–20% of contract value with 100% conversion. **Nothing found in the literature recommends Rung 4 over Rung 2 for a services firm.** The internal docs go straight to Rung 4 without considering Rung 2, which is not in the corpus at all (the research plan lists "Discovery/paid-audit as a standalone product" among its Known Gaps).

**Gate 3 — Rung 2 credit.** If the audit fee is credited against the build, the audit's reference-price benefit is partly surrendered and the buyer's internal reference for the audit becomes zero. Stark's published position on loss-leader vs standalone roadmapping was located but its substance sits behind a video recording; **the trade-off is named in the practitioner corpus and not resolved in anything reachable.**

**Gate 4 — Rung 3/4 → 5 conversion, and it is *contracted at Rung 3/4 or it does not exist*.** Sierra's 100% conversion is attributable in part to having signed paid contracts up front. The internal docs contain no conversion mechanism at all beyond "non-selected applicants enter future funnel."

**Gate 5 — the price-restoration step.** Sitzia & Zizzo say low-then-high is the losing order. If Rung 4 is used, the mechanism by which the *same buyer* is later charged is the unanswered question, and F4.6 is the only instrument here that even sizes it.

### F/36 — Offer Architecture: the six risk-transfer instruments `[DERIVED — P3 §6.2 bonds 5,6,7,18 + JOLT/FOMU + Boulding & Kirmani]`

Each row is an independent lever; they compose. The theoretical basis is Spence's separating condition (cheap now, expensive only if you are bad) and the finding P3 recorded that warranty effects are strongest **precisely for sellers lacking an established reputation** (Boulding & Kirmani 1993, *JCR* 20(1):111–123).

| Instrument | Mechanism | Cost if you are good | Cost if you are bad |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bounded scope, fixed price | removes overrun risk | 0 | overrun absorbed |
| Milestone acceptance gates | buyer controls continuation | 0 | early termination |
| Payment on acceptance | buyer holds the money | working-capital drag | unpaid work |
| Source-code custody / IP-on-final-payment | removes lock-in fear | 0 | nothing to hold |
| Bounded outcome guarantee | signals confidence | 0 | payout |
| Paid pilot before full commitment | small first loss ceiling | small revenue delay | exposed early |

**T-guard (inherited from P3 T3.8): never offer a bond you could not honour.** A bond you cannot pay is not a grey technique; it is misrepresentation with a contract attached.

### F/37 — The Applicant Pool as a Switch-Interview Panel `[SYNTHESIS — P2's handoff, made operational]`

Moesta & Spiek's four forces of progress: **Push** of the current situation, **Pull** of the new solution, **Anxiety** about the new/unproven, **Habit** of the present. Practitioner guidance: **10–15 switch interviews** surface the large majority of actionable patterns. `[PRACTITIONER]`

The campaign's application form already asks five discovery questions. Mapped to forces: "biggest operational problem" and "what happens if unsolved" → **Push**; "top 3 features you wish" → **Pull**; "how is your team currently handling this" → **Habit**. **Anxiety is absent from the form and Anxiety is the force that maps onto FOMU (P4.5) — the mechanism responsible for the majority of no-decision losses.** Adding one question ("what would make you hesitate to hand this to an outside team?") converts the form from a wish-list collector into a four-forces instrument and generates the objection library P5 needs.

Second move: applicants are recent or prospective *switchers* by self-selection. Interviewing 10–15 **non-selected** applicants is a switch-interview panel obtained at zero marginal cost, and it is the cheapest available route out of P2's C2.15 (inductive vs deductive ICP). The consent boundary is in §5-Ethics.

### F/38 — Campaign Architecture: the Two-Track Replacement for the 7-Day Plan `[DERIVED — supersedes B4.3 where the evidence warrants; see C4.6 for what remains contested]`

The 7-day plan's defect is not its content but its **shape**: it assumes a temporal hierarchy of effects (P4.6 says there is little support for one), it optimises for the ~5% in-market (P4.7), and it terminates. Binet & Field's 60/40 (B2B variant **46/54**, LinkedIn B2B Institute with Binet & Field, 2019) is the only budget-split prescription found with a databank behind it, and it partitions **continuous brand-building** against **finite activation** — two tracks running concurrently, not seven days running consecutively.

**Track A — Continuous (Rung 0, Enns' *Educating* rung, the 95%).** Published falsifiable artefacts on a sustained cadence. Content selected by the **Spence pass** (§6.6): if a bad firm could produce it as cheaply, it carries no information. Filters *out* the internal plan's Day 4 (behind-the-scenes, workspace setups, team photos) and Day 3 as written (hackathon reels — externally administered proof of the wrong variable, per P3 C3.17). Filters *in* teardowns with numbers, a named method, and original micro-research (P4.8: 55% of buyers cite strong research and data).

**Track B — Finite activation windows (Rung 1–3, the 5%).** A dated, capacity-bounded offer with a real deadline. This is where the 7-day plan legitimately lives, reduced from "the campaign" to "one activation window." Time-based scarcity has the best meta-analytic support precisely for high-involvement purchases (P4.10).

**Sequencing within Track B is by prospect awareness level (F/32 Ladder A), not by calendar day.** The same window addresses Problem-Aware and Product-Aware prospects with different openings simultaneously; it does not "move the market through" awareness levels over six days.

**What F/38 does not fix.** It does not tell you the split for a firm with zero budget, because the 60/40 and 46/54 splits are *spend* ratios drawn from a databank of firms that had spend. C4.7.

### F/39 — Zero-Budget Access: the Trigger-to-Conversation Chain `[DERIVED — this is the gap P2 and P3 both named and neither could fill]`

Fully specified in **§6.7**. Six stages: **Register → Enrich → Trigger-date → Artefact → Approach → Bond.** The framework's single non-obvious claim is that **the artefact must exist before the approach**, because an approach without one is Enns' bottom rung (a private claim of expertise) while an approach carrying a falsifiable public artefact about the prospect's own situation is a partial borrow from the top rung.

### F/40 — Case-Study Manufacture as a Production Line `[DERIVED — from F/34 plus the win/loss practice corpus]`

Five stations, each with an owner and a gate. (1) **Baseline capture** — before kickoff; gate: a number exists. (2) **Consent capture** — at contract; gate: named attribution, figure publication, reference availability window all signed. (3) **Delta capture** — at acceptance; gate: same instrument as baseline, same method. (4) **Draft and client factual review** — gate: the client confirms the *number*, not the prose. (5) **Publication + reference activation** — gate: the named contact has agreed to a specific call window.

Win/loss practice supplies the timing discipline: interviews should occur **within one to two weeks of the decision**, because memory of the specific moments decays fast; **third-party interviewers** are associated with markedly higher satisfaction with feedback quality (**70% vs 34%**) and higher participation (**70–80% on wins, 50–60% on losses**); **15–20 interviews per quarter** is the reported minimum for patterns, 30–40 for segment-level slicing. `[WEB/VENDOR tier — Clozd 2025 State of Win-Loss and aggregator restatements; the "15–30% revenue increase / up to 50% win-rate improvement" figures circulating in this literature are vendor-published and should not be planned against]`

## 4. Hacks — asymmetric, low-effort, high-leverage

**H4.1 — The regulator's enforcement register is a pre-qualified, pre-triggered, free target list.** Inherited from P2 H2.3 and sharpened: a notified compliance action is simultaneously a CEP, a Critical Event, an observable trigger, *and* a published third-party quantification of the buyer's loss (P2 §7.5: regulated care is the only buyer class where the loss figure is published by someone other than the seller). It is the only item on §6.1 that satisfies all four conditions.

**H4.2 — Publishing the floor does four jobs with one artefact.** A published minimum engagement size + ROI-multiple screen simultaneously: qualifies pre-contact, supplies genuine scarcity framing, sets a non-zero reference price *before any conversation*, and passes the Spence test (a firm that would take any work cannot credibly publish a floor). It is the cheapest available counter-measure to C10 and it costs one web page.

**H4.3 — The non-selected pool is the intelligence asset; the selected one is the cost.** Twenty applications yield twenty Push/Pull/Habit records and (with T4.7) twenty Anxiety records for the price of a form. Three sponsored builds yield three case studies for the price of three builds. F4.5 vs F4.1: the intelligence is roughly free and the proof is expensive, and the internal docs treat them as co-products of the same spend.

**H4.4 — Run the activation window *inside* a competitor's Critical Event.** Where an industry-wide compliance date exists (a statutory deadline, a mandated reporting change), the deadline is externally imposed, publicly verifiable, and identical for every prospect. Scarcity that a regulator enforces is unfakeable, requires no countdown graphic, and is immune to T4.18's exposure.

**H4.5 — Ask the losing applicants why they applied to *you*.** The cheapest available substitute for win/loss analysis at zero deal history. Win/loss practice says interview within 1–2 weeks of the decision; an application rejection is a dated decision moment with a cooperative counterparty.

**H4.6 — One artefact, three rungs.** A single piece of original micro-research (e.g. "we counted store-locator pages and careers-page reconciliation roles across 40 PH multi-branch operators") is a Rung-0 credibility artefact, the payload of a Rung-1 cold approach, and the target list itself. It is the only move found that collapses Enns' ladder.

**H4.7 — Time-box the free diagnostic *in public*.** "45 minutes, and we will tell you whether we are the wrong firm for this" is a bond (it costs you nothing if you are good and costs you the deal if you overreach), a scarcity claim that is trivially true, and a disqualifier all at once.

**Regulatory asymmetry worth stating plainly.** In **Australia**, the new unfair-trading-practices regime (commencing **1 July 2027**) names misleading countdown timers and false low-stock cues explicitly, but applies to **consumer** transactions and to small-business *subscription* contracts (<100 employees or <AUD 10m turnover) — **not** to standard B2B dealings. RA 7394 Ch. VI adds criminal exposure for deliberate false advertising (reported at PHP 500–5,000 plus imprisonment of not less than one month). **The net: the PH regime reaches a B2B campaign that the AU regime's newest instrument does not, while the AU general prohibition reaches it anyway and carries the larger penalties.** `[Practitioner law-firm commentary + ACCC media releases; retrieved 2026-07-28. Not legal advice.]`

### 6.2 — F4.1: Sponsored-Build Cost Ceiling `[DERIVED — extends P1 F16, P2 F2.1]`

```
C_ceiling = ( A_value × p_convert × GP_paid )  −  R_risk

A_value    = artefact-yield multiplier (F4.7), 0–1 scale of the credibility
             substitutes actually obtainable, relative to a full (a)–(d) set
p_convert  = probability the sponsored client or its artefacts produce a paid
             engagement within the payback horizon
GP_paid    = gross profit of the paid engagement so produced
R_risk     = expected reference-price damage (F4.6)
```
**PHP worked:** full artefact set obtainable (`A_value = 1.0`), `p_convert = 0.5` (no basis — see below), `GP_paid = 137,500`, `R_risk = 41,250` (from F4.6 below) →
`C_ceiling = (1.0 × 0.5 × 137,500) − 41,250 = 68,750 − 41,250 =` **PHP 27,500.**
Against a fully-loaded delivery cost of **PHP 90,000**, the sponsored build fails its own ceiling by roughly 3.3×.

**The honest caveat, and it is large.** `p_convert` is the number that decides everything and **no source found measures sponsored-work-to-paid conversion for software services.** Sierra's 100% is for *paid* design partners, which is a different instrument. The law-firm pro bono literature reports the mechanism qualitatively (pro bono counselling to small businesses "can potentially result in these businesses becoming billable clients after they receive financing") but publishes **no conversion rate**. Setting `p_convert = 1.0` and `R_risk = 0` — the internal docs' implicit assumption — gives `C_ceiling = 137,500` and the build passes comfortably. **The formula does not resolve C10; it localises the entire disagreement into two unmeasured parameters.** That is its only real contribution.

### 6.3 — F4.2: Free-Work Payback, extended `[extends P1 F16]`

P1's F16: `N_paid_required = Free_build_cost / GP_per_paid_client` → `90,000 / 137,500 = 0.65`.

Extended to include conversion risk and portfolio-level effects:
```
N_paid_required = ( n_free × C_free ) / ( GP_paid × p_convert )   plus the note below
```
**PHP worked:** 3 sponsored builds × 90,000 = 270,000; `p_convert = 0.5` →
`270,000 / (137,500 × 0.5) = 3.93` paid clients required to repay the campaign.
At `p_convert = 0.25` → **7.85 paid clients.** At `p_convert = 1.0` → 1.96.

### 6.7 — The Zero-Budget Access Method (F/39, fully specified) `[DERIVED — this is the gap P2 and P3 both declared unfilled; it is my construction and it is untested]`

The problem, stated exactly: P2's rubric scores prospects it cannot reach; gate G3 requires reachability and supplies no method; P3's two highest-trust access instruments (referral, reference) both require a prior engagement. Cost constraint: zero budget, zero list.

**Stage 1 — Register.** Choose one vertical where a **public register** exists (regulator enforcement lists, licence registers, association member directories, franchise disclosure documents). Output: an unfiltered universe with names. Cost: zero. Evidence base: P2 H2.3.

**Stage 2 — Enrich from free public surfaces.** Store-locator page → location count (P2 H2.2). Careers page → coordination-role requisitions as a P&L proxy (P2 H2.1). Leadership page + LinkedIn → economic buyer identity and appointment date. Output: the F2.9 inputs, computable without contact. Cost: time only.

**Stage 3 — Trigger-date.** Compute P2's TSS with recency weights. Output: an ordered list where position encodes *timing*, not just fit. **This is the step that makes zero-budget outbound differ from spray: the list is sorted by dated events, and items 1–6 of §6.1 are the only sortable ones.**

**Stage 4 — Artefact first.** Produce one Rung-0 artefact *about the observable pattern across the register*, not about Axon Enjin — e.g. a count of coordination-role requisitions across N operators in the vertical, or a teardown of a publicly visible process failure at a *peer* (T4.9's boundary: peer, not prospect). Output: a falsifiable published document. **This stage is the whole method.** Without it, Stage 5 is Enns' bottom rung — a private claim of expertise, low power. With it, Stage 5 carries third-party-shaped validation borrowed from the top rung (H4.6).

**Stage 5 — Approach, jurisdictionally compliant, single-cue.** Reference the artefact and the prospect's *own dated trigger*. Sender identification, purpose statement, functional unsubscribe, honoured within 5 business days (AU Spam Act); identity/entity/purpose disclosure (PH NPC guidance). Single-cue per T4.14. Expected rates from §6.4: positive reply **0.5–2%** on email, **~28.5–37%** connection acceptance with **~2.2–9.4%** note reply. **Implication: a 200-name trigger-sorted register yields on the order of 1–4 positive email replies and perhaps 60 accepted connections. That is the actual size of zero-budget access, and it is small.**

**Stage 6 — Bond immediately.** The first offer is Rung 1 (time-boxed diagnostic, H4.7) or Rung 2 (paid audit), never Rung 4. Rationale: FOMU is the majority loss mechanism (P4.5), bonds are the cheapest strong signal (P3 §6.2 items 5–7, 18), and Rung 2 avoids the C10 exposure entirely.

**Parallel track, because Stage 5's yield is small.** Items 8, 10, 11, 12 of §6.1 are unobservable, so the register method structurally cannot reach roughly half of live demand. The only instruments found that reach it are Rung-0 publishing (inbound), a16z-style lighthouse elevation (T4.22 — a *person*, no transactional ask), partner/reseller channels bearing reputational risk (P3 §6.2 item 15), and community presence. **None of these is fast, and that is the honest content of the answer to P3's central problem: there is a first move for access, it is Stage 4, and it is a time cost rather than a cash cost.**

### 6.9 — **C10: the zero reference price. What is measured, what is feared.** `[dedicated section, as instructed]`

**The question, restated precisely.** Does delivering a build at zero to a member of the segment you intend to charge (i) lower that buyer's willingness to pay for the next engagement, (ii) lower observers' willingness to pay, and (iii) lower the perceived quality of the work itself?

#### What is actually measured

1. **On services specifically, free depresses perceived quality.** Gorn, Tse & Weinberg (1991, *Marketing Letters* 2:99–110): free-priced services were perceived as **lower in quality** than the same services in a normal price range — and so were exaggerated-price services. `[PRIMARY]` This is direct, on-domain, and it answers (iii) affirmatively. Its limits: 1991, experimental, consumer services, and the paper itself is paywalled (abstract verbatim; full design and means not obtained).
2. **Reference prices form from past observed prices.** Kalyanaram & Winer (1995), a stated empirical generalisation across the reference-price literature. `[PRIMARY]` Answers (i) affirmatively in mechanism.
3. **The low-then-high ordering is the losing one.** Sitzia & Zizzo (2012): high-then-low beat low-then-high on profitability; observing low prices produced a low reference price and a "bad deal" perception on increase. `[PRIMARY]` Limits: laboratory, consumer goods, and specifically products for which subjects had **unclear preferences** — which arguably describes a first-time custom-software buyer well, but the transfer is not tested.
4. **Deep promotion ratchets price sensitivity over years.** Mela, Gupta & Lehmann (1997): 8¼ years of panel data; promotions raise price sensitivity and erode brand equity. `[PRIMARY]` Limits: frequently-purchased packaged goods, aggregate market, not a services firm's individual account.
5. **Zero is discontinuous.** Shampanier, Mazar & Ariely (2007): the 73/27 → 31/69 flip; affect-driven. `[PRIMARY]` Limits: the authors themselves flag low-involvement goods; generalisation to services and to later WTP is not established in the paper.
6. **Free-trial length trades immediate against delayed conversion, and demand cannibalisation is real but not fatal.** Randomised field experiment, **680,588 users, 190 countries, July 2022–July 2024**, 7-day vs 3-day trial: trial adoption **+11.1%**, immediate conversion **not significant**, delayed conversion **+42.4%**, two-year subscription rate **+20.9%**. The authors name the cannibalisation mechanism explicitly — *"users fulfil their primary needs during the trial and reduce their willingness to pay afterward"* — and find it **offset over a longer horizon**. `[PRIMARY, large-N field experiment]` This is the one finding that cuts *against* the fear, and it is the most methodologically robust study in this section.
7. **Free-to-paid conversion rates.** Free-trial models ~**5–20%**; freemium ~**2–5%**. `[WEB/VENDOR tier]` These are software-product rates, not services rates, and the analogy to a bespoke build is weak.

#### What is only feared (asserted, not measured)

- That free work sets a reference price **for observers** rather than only for the recipient. Not measured anywhere found. **This is the most consequential unmeasured proposition in the whole pillar**, because a portfolio of visibly-free builds is precisely what the campaign produces.
- That the sponsored client will refuse to pay later. Asserted widely in practitioner writing (HBR, June 2025: once customers accept free as the reference price it becomes *"difficult—sometimes impossible—to charge later"*; the same piece names undervaluation and abuse as the other two risks, and offers no quantification). `[PRACTITIONER, no data]`
- That "sponsored" framing neutralises the effect. **B4.1's core claim, and nothing found tests it.** The zero-price mechanism Shampanier et al. identify is *affective*, which gives no obvious reason why a naming choice would touch it.
- The pro bono→billable pathway in professional services. The law-firm literature describes it (ABA; pro bono to small businesses converting to billable clients post-financing; corporate clients actively surveying firms' pro bono) but publishes **no conversion rate, no payback period, and no reference-price analysis**. The mechanism there is also different: law-firm pro bono generates goodwill with *corporate clients who are watching*, not with the pro bono recipient. `[PRACTITIONER]`

#### Documented mitigations (each with its source and its cost)

| Mitigation | Source | What it costs |
|---|---|---|
| **Charge something rather than nothing** — Sierra's 10–20% of contract value | First Round / Sierra `[PRACTITIONER, single case]` | Loses the zero-price attention effect; may lose applicants |
| **Substitute a paid audit for the free build** (Rung 2) | Stark `[PRACTITIONER]`; F/35 Gate 2 | Slower artefact acquisition; smaller artefacts |
| **Publish a floor and an ROI screen before any free work** (H4.2) | P2 H2.6; §6.5 | Reduces applicant volume |
| **High-then-low ordering: publish the normal price, then the sponsorship as a stated, dated deviation** | Sitzia & Zizzo `[PRIMARY]` | Requires having a published price — collides with P1's C11 |
| **Non-price consideration instead of money** — baseline data, named reference, publication rights, an introduction (F/34 step 3) | F/34, derived | Extraction burden; possible applicant attrition |
| **Sunset the instrument explicitly** — a numbered, dated cohort that visibly ends | Mela et al. (promotion depth ratchets) `[PRIMARY]` | Requires actually ending it |
| **Longer engagement horizon before judging conversion** | the 680k-user trial experiment `[PRIMARY]` | Delays the payback verdict, worsens cash flow |

#### The verdict

**The risk is real, it is directionally supported by primary evidence, it is supported on services specifically, and it is unquantified for B2B bespoke software.**

- On **(iii) perceived quality**, the evidence is direct and on-domain: Gorn et al. Free lowers perceived service quality. Treat as established for services generally, unreplicated in B2B software.
- On **(i) the recipient's later WTP**, the mechanism is well established (reference-price formation, preference shaping, promotion ratchet) and the magnitude is unknown for this context. The one large modern experiment says demand cannibalisation is **real but offsettable over a two-year horizon** — which is the best available news and it comes from a subscription product, not a bespoke build.
- On **(ii) observers' WTP**, there is **no evidence at all**, and this is where the campaign's actual exposure lies, because the campaign's purpose is to make the free work *visible*.
- **The strongest single argument against Rung 4 is not the reference-price literature — it is that the best-documented practitioner case solving the identical problem (zero logos, need for reference customers, need for roadmap input) charged 10–20% and converted 100%.** There is a documented alternative that carries the same benefits without the exposure, and the internal corpus does not consider it.
- **It is not resolved and should not be recorded as resolved.** The decisive parameters are `p_convert` (F4.1) and observer-side reference-price damage, and neither is measured anywhere reachable. P1 was right to flag C10 as the highest-stakes open question; it remains open, but it is no longer *symmetrically* open — the evidence available leans against free and toward priced-small, and the one counterweight is a subscription-product experiment.

### 6.10 — F4.6: Reference-Price Damage Exposure `[DERIVED — my construction; the parameter is not measured, which is the point]`

```
R_risk = P_next × d × n_exposed

P_next     = the price you intend to charge the same buyer next
d          = proportional WTP reduction attributable to the zero anchor  ← NOT MEASURED
n_exposed  = 1 for the recipient; > 1 if the free work is published as
             representative of what you do
```
**PHP worked (recipient only):** `P_next = 250,000`, `d = 0.15` (an assumed placeholder; there is no measured value), `n_exposed = 1.1` (a token allowance for observer spillover) → `R_risk = 250,000 × 0.15 × 1.1 =` **PHP 41,250** per sponsored build. That figure feeds F4.1 above, where it consumes most of the ceiling.

**Declared honestly: `d` is invented.** F4.6 is not a measurement instrument, it is a **sensitivity harness** — its use is to show that C10's answer is entirely a function of one unmeasured parameter, and to make anyone who wants to proceed state their assumed `d` in writing. At `d = 0`, three sponsored builds are cheap. At `d = 0.3` with `n_exposed = 3`, one sponsored build costs PHP 225,000 in forgone price — more than the build itself.

### 6.11 — F4.5: Applicant-Pool Intelligence Yield `[DERIVED]`

```
Y_intel = A × q_complete × (F_forces / 4)
Patterns_expected = 3 to 5   once  Y_intel ≥ 10
```
`A` = applications received · `q_complete` = share with usable free-text answers · `F_forces` = how many of Push/Pull/Anxiety/Habit the form actually elicits (currently **3 of 4** — Anxiety missing, T4.7).

**Worked:** 19 applications × 0.7 usable × (3/4) = **Y_intel ≈ 10.0** — exactly at Moesta's 10–15 threshold, and only just. Adding the Anxiety question raises the same 19 applications to `19 × 0.7 × 1.0 = 13.3`, i.e. **one extra form field is worth roughly six additional applications' worth of intelligence.** That is the single highest-ratio change identified in this pillar and it costs one line of a Google Form.

### 6.12 — F4.7: Artefact Yield `[DERIVED — implements P3's "design backwards from the artefacts"]`

```
A_value = Σ (w_i × g_i) / Σ w_i

i ∈ {named reference, warm intro commitment, working demo on their data,
     case study with a checkable number}
w_i = P3 §6.2 trust priors:  reference 1.0 · intro 1.0 · demo 0.9 · case study 0.8
g_i = 1 if contractually secured with an acceptance gate, 0.5 if verbally
      agreed, 0 if merely hoped for
```
**Worked, campaign as currently written:** testimonial and portfolio inclusion are contracted, but a testimonial without a name or number is P3 §6.2 item 23 — **near-zero imitability cost, therefore near-zero information.** Scoring: reference 0, intro 0, demo 0.5 (a build exists but not necessarily on their data), case study 0.5 (portfolio inclusion is contracted; a *checkable number* is not) →
`A_value = (1.0×0 + 1.0×0 + 0.9×0.5 + 0.8×0.5) / 3.7 = 0.85 / 3.7 =` **0.23.**
Feeding 0.23 back into F4.1: `C_ceiling = (0.23 × 0.5 × 137,500) − 41,250 =` **negative PHP 25,437.** The campaign as documented has a **negative** cost ceiling — not because sponsored work cannot work, but because the version described contracts for the two weakest artefacts and none of the four strong ones.

### 6.13 — Sub-topics with no formalism, not invented

Per the brief, stated explicitly rather than papered over:

1. **Sponsored-work-to-paid conversion rate for software services.** No source. `p_convert` is a free parameter and everything downstream inherits that.
2. **Observer-side reference-price damage.** No source, no instrument. `n_exposed` and `d` are both invented.
3. **Content-ratio prescriptions.** No formalism exists and none should be constructed. Binet & Field's 60/40 and the B2B 46/54 are **spend** splits from the IPA Databank and do not transfer to topic mixes (C4.7).
4. **Optimal number of sponsored builds.** Sierra targeted 4 from a 40-name list and landed 6; Moesta's interview guidance is 10–15; neither is a formula and the two numbers answer different questions.
5. **Awareness-level distribution in a market.** Schwartz gives no measurement method. Any "40% of our market is Problem Aware" claim would be fabricated.
6. **Cost of a reference-price restoration.** F/35 Gate 5 names the decision; no source prices the move. P1's F19 price-increase ladder (`g ∈ 0.08–0.15` with notice) assumes an existing paid relationship, not a zero base — it cannot be applied from zero, because the multiplier is undefined at `P_old = 0`. **That is a small but exact arithmetic statement of why C10 matters: the price-increase instrument the corpus already owns literally does not function from a base of free.**
7. **Ethical-line quantification.** The legitimate/grey/misrepresentation boundary is a legal and normative judgement. No formula, and the labelling in §5 is argued, not computed.

## 8. Contradictions — stated as open questions, deliberately unresolved

**C4.1 — The rename does not touch the mechanism.** B4.1 holds that calling it a "Sponsored MVP Build Sprint" produces exclusivity and professional positioning. Shampanier et al. locate the zero-price effect in **affect**, and Gorn et al. find the quality penalty attaches to the *price level*, not the label. *Open question: is there any evidence that framing moderates the zero-price quality penalty for services? None was found.*

**C4.2 — The one large modern experiment cuts against the reference-price fear.** The 680k-user trial experiment finds demand cannibalisation real but **offset over two years**. The reference-price literature (Sitzia & Zizzo, Mela et al.) says the damage persists. *Open question: is the difference explained by product class (subscription software vs bespoke service), by horizon, or by the fact that a trial is time-bounded while a delivered free build is permanent?*

**C4.3 — The best-documented case both supports and undermines the campaign.** Sierra proves an early-customer program can convert at 100% and yield roadmap input plus references. It did so **by charging**. *Open question: is the 100% attributable to the payment, to the screening (40 → 6), to the cadence, or to survivorship in who writes case studies about themselves?*

**C4.4 — Free work as marketing has a professional-services precedent whose mechanism does not transfer.** Law-firm pro bono is documented as business development, but the buyer being influenced is the *watching corporate client*, not the pro bono recipient. Axon Enjin's campaign expects the recipient to convert. *Open question: is there any pro bono literature where the recipient converts, and at what rate? Not found.*

**C4.5 — Scarcity is evidenced for exactly this product class, and the campaign cannot currently claim it honestly.** Time-based scarcity is the best-supported form for high-involvement purchases; the internal docs use it; neither doc states the capacity basis. *Open question left to the operator, not to research: what is the real number of slots and what happens to a strong late application?*

**C4.6 — F/38 replaces a sequence with two tracks, and the replacement is only as good as its own evidence.** Vakratsas & Ambler undermine the *sequence*; they do not license my two-track structure, which is assembled from Binet & Field (spend ratios from firms with budgets) and Ehrenberg-Bass (95:5, itself an arithmetic derivation). *Open question: what is the brand/activation split for a firm whose only currency is founder hours? The databank contains no such firms.*

**C4.7 — The 40/30/20/10 content ratio has no evidentiary basis and neither does any replacement.** *Open question: is there any published test of content-topic mix effects? None found at any tier. The honest position is that the ratio is a planning convenience.*

**C4.8 — Enns' ladder and Sierra's sourcing rule disagree about cold approach.** Enns places Selling (cold email, cold calls) at the bottom rung: a private claim, low power. Sierra deliberately avoided warm introductions because *"strangers hold you to higher standards."* *Open question: does cold sourcing produce weaker access but better-qualified partners, and are those the same axis or two?*

**C4.9 — The channels in the internal docs do not reach the buyer P2 selected.** Facebook/Instagram/TikTok/Discord/startup groups reach MSME owners; P2's thesis is a multi-branch mid-market ops leader, and P1's AU reading targets a market these channels do not serve organically. *Open question inherited and unresolved across three pillars: is the buyer the PH MSME owner (in which case P2's rubric is the wrong instrument) or the mid-market ops leader (in which case the channel plan is the wrong instrument)? The corpus has never chosen.*

**C4.10 — DITF's own literature disagrees with itself about which number belongs to which outcome.** The abstract-derived figures (verbal r = .126, behavioural r = .052 ns) and the University at Buffalo release (which labels .126 as behavioural and calls it small) cannot both be right. *Left unresolved: the abstract was inaccessible (403 from both the publisher and ResearchGate). The qualitative finding — verbal yes, behavioural no — is consistent across both.*

**C4.11 — Two readings of Spam Act inferred consent, one permissive and one not.** Vendor commentary asserts that a conspicuously published work email address suffices for inferred consent in B2B. The law-firm reading (DLA Piper) states inferred consent should only be relied on where an **existing commercial relationship** relating to the subject matter exists, and that ACMA favours express consent. *Open question with legal consequences: which reading governs a first cold email to a named ops director? Not resolved here, and the tier difference between the two sources is large.*

**C4.12 — Free is bad for quality perception *and* expensive is bad for quality perception.** Gorn et al. found the penalty at both ends. This sits directly on top of P1's C8 (high anchoring under a credibility deficit) and P3 §6.6. *Open question: is there a "normal range" for a young cross-border studio, and if the range itself is what generates trust, then both the sponsored build and the premium anchor are outside it.*

**C4.13 — The applicant pool is both an intelligence asset and a consent problem.** F/37 and H4.5 extract research value from people who applied for a build, not for a study. *Open question: does the PH DPA's consent framework permit research use of application free-text without a disclosed research purpose? The safe reading is no; the campaign's form contains no such disclosure.*

**C4.14 — Every rate in the conversion cascade is vendor-published and the structure is mine.** *Open question: what is the actual first-campaign cascade for a firm with no list? Unknowable in advance; F4.3 is an effort-sizing device masquerading as a forecast if used carelessly.*

**C4.15 — The 95:5 figure is load-bearing in F/38 and is a derivation, not a measurement.** *Open question: has anyone measured in-market share directly, rather than inferring it from assumed replacement cycles?*

**C4.16 — Reciprocity's durability claim is web-tier and I relied on it in T4.16.** *Open question: is the "effects decay within a week" claim traceable to a primary meta-analysis? I could not verify it.*

### Verdict on C10

**C10 is real, directionally evidenced, on-domain for services, and unquantified for B2B bespoke software. It is not overstated, and it is not proven either.**

The precise state of knowledge: free provision demonstrably lowers *perceived service quality* (Gorn et al., primary, on services); reference prices demonstrably form from observed past prices (Kalyanaram & Winer, primary); the low-then-high ordering is demonstrably the losing one where preferences are unclear (Sitzia & Zizzo, primary); deep promotion demonstrably ratchets price sensitivity over years (Mela et al., primary). Four primaries, four independent mechanisms, all pointing the same way. Against that, one large well-designed field experiment (680,588 users) finds demand cannibalisation real but **offset over a two-year horizon** — for a subscription product, on a time-bounded trial, which is not the instrument in question.

**What tips it is not the reference-price literature but the existence of a documented alternative.** Sierra faced the identical problem — no logos, needing reference customers, needing roadmap input, needing to convert early users — and solved it by charging 10–20% of contract value, screening 40 names to 6, and converting 100%. Rung 2 and Rung 3 of F/35 deliver the same four credibility artefacts P3 identified as the reason for the campaign, and they set a **positive** reference price rather than a zero one. The internal corpus never considered Rung 2 because paid discovery is one of the research plan's own Known Gaps.

**And the arithmetic that makes the risk concrete rather than rhetorical:** P1's own price-increase instrument, F19 (`P_new = P_old × (1 + g)`), is undefined at `P_old = 0`. The corpus already owns a tool for raising prices and that tool does not function from a base of free. Whatever the mechanism for charging the sponsored client later turns out to be, it is not in the corpus.

**Two things must be true for the campaign as written to be sound, and neither is measured:** `p_convert` must be high, and observer-side damage (`d`, `n_exposed` in F4.6) must be near zero. F4.1 and F4.7 exist to force those assumptions into writing. Scored against the campaign as currently documented — which contracts for a testimonial and portfolio inclusion but not for a named reference, a warm introduction, or a checkable number — `A_value = 0.23` and the cost ceiling is **negative**. That is not a verdict against sponsored work. It is a verdict against *this specification* of sponsored work, and it is fixable at the level of the contract rather than the strategy.
