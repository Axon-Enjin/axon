You're asking the right question, but I think your current model is missing the most important factor.

**Clients do not buy hours. They buy outcomes.**

If Axon Enjin charges based only on effort, you'll always be competing against freelancers. If you charge based on business value, you compete as a partner.

I'd reframe your pricing model like this:

> **Price = Business Value + Complexity + Risk + Cost + Future Commitment**

Let's dissect your insights.

### 1. Human × Effort × Hours = Price

This is your **minimum floor**, not your selling price.

Suppose a website takes:

* 80 hours
* 2 developers
* Internal cost = ₱35,000

That does **not** mean you charge ₱35,000.

Ask instead:

> "What is this worth to the client?"

If that website helps them close a ₱5M client...

Charging ₱50,000 is actually cheap.

---

### 2. Perspective matters. If they believe it, they'll buy it.

Correct.

People don't understand code.

They understand:

* "We'll automate your manual process."
* "You'll save 40 hours every month."
* "You'll respond to customers instantly."
* "You'll eliminate encoding mistakes."

Sell transformation.

Not technology.

---

### 3. Loss Aversion

One of the strongest principles.

Don't say

> "This system will improve efficiency."

Say

> "Every month you're losing around 60 staff hours doing this manually."

or

> "Without centralized inventory, stock discrepancies continue to cost money."

People move faster to stop bleeding than to gain something new.

---

### 4. "You can fake the number of devs"

I would not recommend this.

You don't need to lie.

Instead, increase perceived capability.

For example:

Instead of

> "I'm a solo developer."

Say

> "Axon Enjin handles design, architecture, development, QA, deployment, and maintenance."

That's true if you have processes or trusted collaborators. Misrepresenting team size can damage trust if discovered, especially with business clients.

---

### 5. Tech Cost

Absolutely.

Include:

* APIs
* AI models
* Cloud hosting
* Database
* Storage
* Email
* SMS
* Domains
* Licenses

These should either be billed separately or incorporated into recurring fees.

---

### 6. Maintenance Cost

Never forget this.

Clients assume software lasts forever.

It doesn't.

Maintenance includes:

* bug fixes
* security updates
* server monitoring
* backups
* dependency updates
* API changes
* feature improvements

This is recurring revenue.

# Here's the model I would use for Axon Enjin

## Layer 1. Cost Floor

Know your actual cost.

* Labor
* Infrastructure
* Third-party services
* Operations
* Taxes
* Buffer

Never price below this.

---

## Layer 2. Complexity Multiplier

Ask:

* How many integrations?
* How many users?
* AI involved?
* Mobile?
* Payment systems?
* Security requirements?

Each increases the multiplier.

Simple website

×1

Business dashboard

×1.5

Enterprise platform

×2–3

---

## Layer 3. Value Multiplier

This is where startups make money.

Ask:

> If this project succeeds...

* How much revenue will they generate?
* How much cost will they save?
* How much time will they save?
* How much risk disappears?

If software saves a company ₱2M annually...

Charging ₱300,000 may be a bargain.

---

## Layer 4. Risk Premium

If the client wants:

* two-week deadline
* unclear requirements
* many revisions
* government compliance
* mission-critical deployment

Increase price.

Higher uncertainty deserves higher compensation.

---

## Layer 5. Strategic Premium

Sometimes you're not building software.

You're building competitive advantage.

Example:

An AI platform that automates an entire workflow.

You're not selling code.

You're selling leverage.

Leverage is expensive.

---

# I also recommend separating your pricing into three buckets.

### 1. Discovery & Strategy

Paid.

Requirements gathering.

Business analysis.

System architecture.

Clients who won't pay for planning usually don't value execution either.

---

### 2. Development

One-time project fee.

Not hourly.

Clients want certainty.

---

### 3. Maintenance

Monthly subscription.

Include things like:

* Hosting
* Monitoring
* Updates
* Bug fixes
* Minor improvements
* Technical support

This creates predictable recurring revenue.

---

# One principle I learned early

Never let clients calculate your hourly rate.

If they know it took you 20 hours instead of 60, they'll think you overcharged.

In reality, they're paying for the years it took you to become capable of finishing it in 20 hours.

# For Axon Enjin, I would position you differently.

Don't say:

> "We build software."

Say:

> **"We engineer business systems that help organizations operate faster, make better decisions, and scale with confidence."**

That's a different category. You're no longer selling development. You're selling operational transformation.

One last thought.

Many founders believe pricing is about recovering costs. Mature companies know pricing is about capturing value. Costs determine the minimum you can charge. Value determines the maximum the market will willingly pay. The art is finding that range and pricing confidently within it.
