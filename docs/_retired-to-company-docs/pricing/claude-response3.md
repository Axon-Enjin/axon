Your advisor is identifying a real problem but prescribing the wrong fix. The answer isn't to be slower. It's that you're currently treating speed as something you *reveal* rather than something you *sell*.

Start by separating three things that are getting conflated:

**Responsiveness** — how fast you reply, turn around questions, fix things. Always be fast here. It costs nothing and it's your single biggest advantage as an offshore vendor, because the buried fear every Australian client has is that you'll go quiet. Never dampen this.

**Delivery speed** — how fast the work ships. This is a genuine competitive asset, but it has to be priced, not given away.

**Speed of committing** — how fast you promise in the room. *This* is where your advisor is right, and it's the only one you should slow down.

## The core error

When you say "I can have that done by Friday," you've described your effort. The client now prices your work against three days of labor. When you say "you'd be live before your peak season instead of after it — that's a quarter of revenue you're currently planning to lose," you've described their outcome, and the three days never enter the conversation.

Same speed. Completely different pricing conversation. This is the same problem as cost-plus from earlier: any time your input becomes visible, you've handed them a cheaper thing to pay for.

## Give the speed a cause

"It's fast because it's simple" is a discount message. "It's fast because we've built this fourteen times and 60% of it already exists" is a premium message. Identical delivery date.

This also solves the ratchet your advisor is worried about. Unexplained speed becomes the permanent baseline. Speed with an attributed cause is controllable — you can say "that project reused our existing booking module; this one needs a custom ERP integration, so it's eight weeks." That's a credible, specific reason, and it holds. You lose that ability entirely if you've let them believe you're just fast at everything.

## Never quote a timeline in the meeting

Quote after you've scoped, in writing, and quote a commitment rather than a capability. The gap between what you *can* do and what you *commit to* is your buffer — that's not sandbagging, every construction and engineering firm on earth works this way. Then deliver early sometimes, as a gift rather than as the standard.

## Charge for it explicitly

The cleanest inversion available to you: make speed a paid tier. Standard delivery in ten weeks, expedited in five for +35%. The moment speed has a price on a page, it stops reading as "easy" and starts reading as valuable. Some clients will pay it, and the ones who don't have just anchored on the idea that your speed is worth money.

Stronger version if your delivery is genuinely reliable: attach a guarantee. Live by a fixed date or you credit back 10%. That converts speed into risk you're absorbing on their behalf, which is unambiguously worth paying for and which slower competitors can't offer. Only do this once you trust your estimates.

## The part your advisor may actually be sensing

There's a credibility cost to fast promises that's separate from the pricing one. A buyer who's been burned before hears "two weeks" and doesn't think *efficient* — they think *this person doesn't understand my problem yet*. Multi-branch operations have messy legacy data and undocumented workarounds, and the buyer knows it even if you don't yet. An instant confident timeline can read as naive rather than capable.

Eagerness also signals slack capacity. "We can start Monday" tells them nobody else is hiring you. "We can start the 15th" doesn't.

So: be visibly fast at responding, be quietly fast at delivering, and be slow, deliberate, and specific about what you commit to. The behavior your advisor is reacting to is the last one — it's a sales-discipline problem wearing a pricing-problem costume.
