Your list has three good instincts, two line items, and one thing I'd cut. Let me go through it.

## The core problem with #1

`Human × Effort × Hours = Price` is your **floor**, not your price. Cost-plus pricing is the most common trap for services businesses because it ties your revenue to your inefficiency — get faster and better, and you earn less. It also invites the client to argue about your inputs ("why does this take 200 hours?") instead of your outcome.

Use it as a sanity check. Calculate your fully loaded cost — salaries, benefits, tools, rent, admin time, and realistically ~60-70% billable utilization, not 100% — and that gives you a break-even day rate. Anything below it, you walk. That's all cost-plus is for.

Price the project against **what it's worth to them**. A booking system that recovers 15% of no-shows for a clinic doing ₱4M/year is worth a lot more than 200 hours of your time, and it's worth more to that clinic than to a smaller one. Same build, different price, and that's correct.

## #2 and #3 are real, but they're framing, not pricing

Perception and loss aversion are how you *communicate* a price you've already set on solid ground. Done well, that means making the cost of the status quo concrete: the hours their staff burn on manual entry, the leads dropping off a broken form, the downtime, the churn. That's not manipulation — most clients genuinely haven't quantified what standing still costs them. You're doing arithmetic they skipped.

The practical mechanic worth adopting: present **three options**, not one price. Something lean, your recommended tier, and a premium version. This anchors the middle, shifts the conversation from "yes or no" to "which one," and tells you a lot about their real budget from which way they lean.

## #4 — drop this one

Faking headcount isn't a pricing strategy, it's a misrepresentation, and it's the kind that unravels in specific, expensive ways. Clients ask for standups. They ask who's on the project. They ask for a team intro when a deadline slips. They talk to your other clients. If a dispute ever gets formal, your internal records are discoverable and you'll have signed statements of work that don't match reality — that turns a contract argument into a fraud argument.

And it's unnecessary, because the legitimate version gets you the same outcome: **just don't itemize your inputs at all.** Quote a fixed fee for a defined scope and deliverable. "₱850,000 for the platform, delivered in 12 weeks, these features, this SLA." You are not obligated to disclose your cost structure, your team size, or how you built it. Declining to break down headcount is completely normal and no client will blink. Inventing headcount is a different act entirely.

If you're being pressured for a breakdown, that's usually a signal the client is buying labor rather than outcomes — which is a lower-margin relationship you may not want.

## #5 and #6 are right, but restructure them

Tech cost and maintenance shouldn't be buried in the build price. Split them:

- **Pass-through infra** (cloud, APIs, licenses) — bill at cost or with a small management margin, stated separately. Protects you when their usage triples.
- **Maintenance as a recurring retainer** — a common benchmark is 15-20% of build cost annually, scoped clearly: what's covered (bug fixes, security patches, dependency updates, X hours of small changes) and what isn't (new features → change order).

That retainer is the most valuable thing in your pricing model. Project revenue is lumpy and every month starts at zero. Recurring revenue is what makes the business survivable and what makes it worth something if you ever sell it.

## What's missing from your list

**Scope control.** Most agencies don't lose money on bad pricing, they lose it on scope creep they never billed for. A written change-order process — "that's outside scope, here's what it adds in time and cost, approve?" — protects your margin more than any pricing formula.

**Payment terms.** Deposit up front (30-50%), milestone payments, and you own the IP until final payment clears. Non-negotiable.

**Raising prices.** Your rate should go up as your portfolio does. Most people underprice for years past the point they needed to.

One thing that would sharpen this considerably: are you selling mainly to local clients or to overseas ones? The pricing logic diverges a lot — if you're serving US or Australian clients, you're competing against their local agencies' rates, and cost-plus pricing leaves an enormous amount of money on the table.
