Your advisor gave you a warning that many young agencies learn the hard way.

The answer isn't "don't be fast."

The answer is **don't sell speed as the product. Sell reliability. Let speed become a pleasant surprise.**

There is a psychological principle at play.

Imagine two agencies.

**Agency A**

> "We'll finish in 7 days."

They deliver in 7 days.

Client reaction:

> "As expected."

No delight. You've simply met the promise.

Now imagine something goes wrong and it takes 10 days.

The client feels disappointed because you broke your promise.

Now consider:

**Agency B**

> "We'll deliver within 14 days."

They deliver in 7.

Client reaction:

> "Wow."

Same work.

Different expectation.

One creates satisfaction. The other creates disappointment.

---

## Speed also changes perceived value.

Suppose a lawyer solves a legal problem in 15 minutes.

Most people don't think:

> "They're amazing."

Some think:

> "That's it? Why am I paying so much?"

The same happens in software.

If you tell a client,

> "We'll build your system in three days."

They may unconsciously conclude:

* It must be easy.
* It can't be thorough.
* Why is it expensive if it only took three days?

They're paying for expertise, but they're estimating value using time.

---

## Your competitive advantage shouldn't be "We're fast."

It should be:

> "We execute predictably."

Businesses love predictability.

They hate uncertainty.

Instead of saying:

> "We'll finish in one week."

Say:

> "Our implementation process is designed to minimize delays while maintaining quality. Based on the current scope, our target delivery is two weeks."

Notice the difference.

The first is a promise.

The second is a professional forecast.

---

## Keep your internal speed.

This is actually one of Axon Enjin's biggest advantages.

If AI, automation, reusable components, and strong engineering let you deliver in half the time, **keep that advantage internal.**

Your client doesn't need to know every efficiency you've built.

That's your intellectual capital.

Toyota doesn't tell customers exactly how many manufacturing optimizations allow them to produce cars efficiently. Those efficiencies increase profit and resilience.

---

## Underpromise. Overdeliver.

This advice is repeated because it works.

Not because clients like waiting.

Because expectations drive satisfaction.

If you consistently estimate four weeks and deliver in three, clients begin to trust your estimates and are pleasantly surprised.

If you consistently promise one week and deliver in ten days, clients begin to doubt your word.

Trust compounds.

---

## Be careful with "brusko."

I think your advisor may not have been criticizing your speed.

They may have been criticizing your confidence without enough context.

When an experienced consultant hears:

> "Easy."

or

> "We'll finish that quickly."

They often think:

> "You haven't discovered the hidden complexity yet."

Experienced firms know that software projects change.

Requirements evolve.

Unexpected edge cases appear.

Integrations fail.

Clients request revisions.

A little humility in estimates signals experience, not weakness.

---

## Here's what I'd say instead.

Instead of:

> "We'll have it done in one week."

Say:

> "Based on what we know today, we estimate one to two weeks. Once discovery is complete, we'll provide a more precise timeline."

That tells the client:

* You have a process.
* You understand uncertainty.
* You don't make careless promises.

---

## A strategy I'd encourage Axon Enjin to adopt

Build your reputation around three things:

1. **Clarity**: Clients always know what's happening.
2. **Reliability**: Your commitments are consistently met.
3. **Velocity**: When possible, you deliver ahead of schedule.

Notice that speed comes last.

It's still a competitive advantage, but it's experienced rather than advertised.

One final thought.

Speed is easy for competitors to claim. Anyone can say, "We build software fast." Much harder to imitate is a reputation where clients say, "When Axon Enjin gives a timeline, they hit it. And sometimes they finish earlier than expected."

That's the kind of reputation that lets you raise prices over time.
