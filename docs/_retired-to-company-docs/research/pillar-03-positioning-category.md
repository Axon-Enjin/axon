## 1. Pillars — load-bearing principles

**P3.1 — Positioning is the choice of comparison set; everything else follows from it.** `[ASSERTED — Dunford, primary practitioner; corroborated in mechanism by price-anchoring literature]`
Dunford's own words: *"Your positioning context sets off a really powerful set of assumptions about who your product competes with, what features your product should have, who the product is intended for, and even things like what the product should cost."* Her worked illustration is a CRM: tell a buyer your product is a CRM and, with Salesforce as the reference, *"you would assume my product costs less than that."* The first of her five components is **competitive alternatives**, defined by the question *"What would a customer do if your offering didn't exist?"* — which is **structurally identical to Pillar 1's EVE step 1 (Competitive Reference Value)**. Positioning and pricing are therefore not two decisions; the positioning choice *is* the selection of the reference price. This is the single most load-bearing finding in the pillar and it is the mechanism that makes B2 correct in form even where it is unproven in effect.

**P3.2 — Undercutting on price converts a cost advantage into a quality objection, and positioning is the only lever that resolves it.** `[EVIDENCED in the price-quality half; ASSERTED in the resolution half]`
Pillar 1 P1.6 established the price → perceived-quality inference (Rao & Monroe 1989, primary tier) and that prices at the *low* end of a range trigger quality concern. Pillar 1 C5 then noted the studio's structural bind: price near the PH cost base and read as low quality; price near AU agency rates and forfeit the only structural advantage. The resolution the positioning literature offers is not a price move at all: it is to change the reference set so that the same absolute number sits at a *different* point in a *different* range. A PHP 600,000 fee is cheap against an AU digital agency, expensive against a PH freelancer, and *not comparable* against a hired operations manager's annual salary. B6's "50–65% of local agency pricing" is a within-frame answer to a frame-selection problem. **No source found tests whether frame substitution actually neutralises the low-price quality inference.** Recorded as C3.4.

**P3.3 — The economics of specialization run through pattern repetition, not through preference.** `[ASSERTED — Baker, Morgan; directionally corroborated by Hinge survey data]`
Morgan's formulation: *"Your narrow focus exposes you to more repetitions of similar problems in a shorter timeframe than your generalist competitors will have access to."* Baker's is that expertise develops through concentrated focus, that narrow specialization improves **pattern recognition**, and that positioning *"provides justification for charging more, with clients prepared to pay for expertise that's difficult to find elsewhere"* — he grounds the price claim in **inelastic demand for scarce expertise**. `claude-response2.md`'s B4 is the same argument stated operationally, and it adds the estimation consequence: repetition is what lets you *"quote a fixed fee without fear."* Note the chain: specialization → repetition → pattern recognition → confident fixed-fee estimation → the ability to price on value rather than on hours. That chain, not the price premium itself, is the load-bearing part, because it is the only thing in the pillar that connects positioning to Pillar 1's F/2 (price-led costing) and Pillar 6 (delivery discipline).

**P3.4 — Positioning is a market fact; messaging is a communication choice; the corpus conflates them.** `[ASSERTED — Dunford, Morgan]`
Dunford: *"Positioning is not equivalent to messaging. It isn't a tagline."* Morgan splits the same territory differently and more usefully for a services firm: **specialization** is *"the decision you make about how to focus your business"* — an act of the firm — whereas **positioning** is *"the process of seeking and earning a specific, desired location in a market."* The verb is *earn*. Under Morgan's definition a firm cannot unilaterally position itself; it can only specialize and then wait for the market to concede the position. He notes explicitly that this is why services differ from products: *"services build reputation slowly, unlike products."* **B3 is a messaging change.** Changing "We build software" to "We engineer business systems" alters what the firm says; it does not alter what buyers compare it against, which is the actual definition of positioning in P3.1. Whether the sentence change causes the frame change is C3.2.

**P3.5 — Credibility deficit is a *signaling* problem with a formal solution structure, and the solution requires the signal to be costly.** `[EVIDENCED — Spence 1973, primary; warranty-signal experiments, primary]`
Spence's 1973 *Job Market Signaling* is the canonical treatment of exactly Axon Enjin's situation: a party whose quality is unobservable to the buyer, in a market where low-quality parties have every incentive to make the same claims. The key axiom, stated as found: **for a signal to be informative its cost must be negatively correlated with quality — sufficiently expensive that low-quality imitators find imitation prohibitive.** This yields a *separating equilibrium*. The operational consequence for a credibility-substitute catalogue is severe and counter-intuitive: **any credibility substitute that a low-quality competitor could produce at the same cost carries no information and therefore generates no trust.** A testimonial page is cheap for everyone. A published, falsifiable benchmark that would embarrass you if wrong is not. §6.2 ranks the catalogue on exactly this axis.

**P3.6 — Category choice moves decision-chain length, and therefore moves the deal economics directly.** `[DERIVED from P2's C2.4 + Dunford's assumption-set claim; no source found tests it]`
Pillar 2 handed this over as its first note. The chain: Dunford (P3.1) says the category sets assumptions about *who the product is for* and *what it should cost*. Pillar 2's F2.7 makes cycle length a function of `DCL = S_approvals + P_paper`. If a category label is what tells a buyer whether a purchase is an operational tool (ops leader decides) or an IT system (CTO, security review, procurement, DPA), then the label is an input to `DCL`, hence to `Cycle_est_days`, hence to Pillar 2's F2.8 `PCR` and F2.10 `EVP`. That makes the "software house vs operations partner" choice measurable in days and in expected value rather than merely rhetorical. §6.5 formalises it as F3.6. **This is my derivation. No source found measures decision-chain length as a function of vendor category self-description.** C3.7.

**P3.7 — In compliance-driven categories the value figure is established by legislation, not by your track record — the only class where credibility substitutes are not load-bearing for the value claim.** `[EVIDENCED for the penalty magnitudes via Pillar 2 §7.5; DERIVED for the positioning consequence]`
Pillar 2 P2.10 established that regulated care is the only buyer class where the loss figure is published by a third party. The positioning consequence is specific: in every other category, a young firm's value claim requires a credibility substitute to be believed at all ("how do you know you'll save us PHP 40k/month?"). In a compliance category the claim is *"the Act says AUD 412,500 to AUD 15M"* — a statement whose truth is independent of the speaker. This does **not** solve the *delivery*-credibility problem ("can you actually build it?"), only the *value*-credibility problem. Conflating the two is the trap. C3.9.

**P3.8 — Firm youth and firm smallness are separable, empirically real hazards — and the mechanism named in the founding literature is *trust*, which is the same variable positioning operates on.** `[EVIDENCED, with contested magnitude]`
Stinchcombe's 1965 **liability of newness** holds that new organizations fail at higher rates than old ones, and the stated mechanism is that *"new organizations are vulnerable because their participants are strangers and efficient organization requires trust among members; trust takes time to build."* Note this is internally-directed trust, and its extension to *buyer*-directed trust is an inference, not Stinchcombe's claim. The construct spawned **liability of smallness** and **liability of adolescence**; the literature reports that newness and smallness generally co-occur and that *"the effect of newness on death rates results often stronger."* The evidence is **contested**: Brüderl & Schüssler's test on West German firms found the newness hypothesis *"not a good representation of the mortality hazard"* and substituted a **liability of adolescence** — an inverted-U hazard peaking between roughly one and fifteen years after founding, on the reasoning that initial resource endowments buy a honeymoon before the hazard bites. Other recent work confirms newness and reports *"a much higher risk of failure in organizations' early lifetime than rates found in previous research."* This is a genuine, unresolved academic disagreement and it is preserved as C3.10. What survives either way: **firm youth is a real hazard with a trust mechanism, and buyers who discount young firms are not being irrational.**

**P3.9 — Specialist proliferation is a predictable consequence of generalist concentration, not merely a marketing preference.** `[EVIDENCED — Carroll's resource-partitioning theory, primary organizational ecology]`
Resource-partitioning theory: *"in heavily concentrated populations, specialist organizations arise and proliferate"* — the increased dominance of large generalists **enhances the life chances of specialists**, because as generalists concentrate toward the homogeneous centre of the resource space, *"there is an increasingly large resource space that lies outside their target areas, allowing new forms to take advantage of those unclaimed resources."* Tested on Dutch daily newspapers 1968–1994 and on US brewing (the microbrewery result). This is the closest thing to *academic* support for niching down that exists, and it is importantly **not** the consultant argument: it does not say specialists earn premiums, it says specialists **survive** where generalists cannot reach. The condition is a concentrated generalist population and a heterogeneous periphery. Whether AU mid-market operational software satisfies that condition is untested and is C3.11.

**P3.10 — There is a serious, well-evidenced counter-position holding that the entire differentiation/category-design programme is misdirected.** `[EVIDENCED — Ehrenberg-Bass programme; itself contested]`
The Ehrenberg-Bass Institute's position, in their own words: *"customers don't care much if your brand is different from the competition"* and *"Customers aren't that convinced or concerned if brands are different. They will naturally buy the brands they remember."* Their prescription inverts the canon: *"Think 'Hey, we're here, we're good, give us a try!' rather than 'We are different from other brands.'"* They separate two things the positioning canon fuses:
- **Differentiation** — *"features of a product or service that differentiate a brand in the eyes of the customer"*; about *meaning*.
- **Distinctiveness** — *"making your brand easily identified by customers. Brand names, logos, jingles, slogans and house styles contribute to this"*; about *memory*.
Their claim is that growth comes from **mental availability** (*"the brand being easily thought of in buying situations"*) plus **physical availability** (*"easy to find and buy"*), and they insist it is *"Mental **and** Physical availability, not Mental **or** Physical Availability."* They report the **Double Jeopardy** pattern holds in B2B — *"a growing B2B brand attracts many more buyers who buy slightly more often"* — and that *"brand rejection is low and rarely a barrier to growth."* The B2B mechanism they name is **category entry points**: linking the brand in memory to buying situations such as *"contract renewal," "compliance audits" or "IT infrastructure upgrades."* This is treated as a genuine challenge, not a footnote — see C3.3, which is the most consequential contradiction in the pillar.

**P3.11 — Every credibility substitute is either a bond or a burn, and the two behave differently.** `[EVIDENCED in structure — signaling literature; DERIVED in application]`
The signaling literature distinguishes signals that put something at risk contingent on non-performance (a warranty, a guarantee, a penalty clause — the cost is incurred *only if* you are bad) from signals that consume resources up front regardless of outcome (an expensive office, a published research study, an open-source library — the cost is *sunk* whether or not you perform). Boulding & Kirmani (1993, *Journal of Consumer Research*) tested warranty length, warranty scope, **and the credibility of the warrantor**, and found consumer responses *"generally consistent with the behavioral assumptions of signaling theory"* — critically establishing that a signal's effect is **conditional on the signaller being credible enough for the bond to be believed**. And the finding that matters most for a firm with no reputation: *"numerous studies have demonstrated positive warranty effects for products that lack an established brand reputation."* The catalogue in §6.2 separates bonds from burns because they fail differently — a bond fails when the buyer doubts you could honour it; a burn fails when it is cheap to fake.

**P3.12 — The offshore trust discount is a documented, measured effect, and it is *larger on quality judgement than on purchase intention*, and it *shrinks when other cues are present*.** `[EVIDENCED — Peterson & Jolibert 1995 meta-analysis, primary]`
This is the single most operationally useful empirical finding in the pillar. Peterson & Jolibert's meta-analysis (52 articles, 69 independent studies, 1,520 effect sizes, *JIBS* 26:883–900) reports:
- average effect size on **quality/reliability perceptions = .30**;
- average effect size on **purchase intentions = .19**;
- **verbal product descriptions produced larger COO effects than the presence of an actual product**;
- **single-cue studies produced larger COO effects than multiple-cue studies**;
- COO effects are *"only somewhat generalizable."*
Verlegh & Steenkamp's independent 1999 review and meta-analysis (*Journal of Economic Psychology* 20:521–546) reaches the same ordering: *"country of origin has a larger effect on perceived quality than on attitude toward the product or purchase intention."* Three consequences, all directly actionable and all derived rather than tested-in-services: (a) the discount is real but its effect on *whether they buy* is roughly two-thirds of its effect on *what they think of you*; (b) **the single-cue/multiple-cue finding is the academic warrant for the entire credibility-substitute catalogue** — every additional diagnostic cue you supply reduces the weight the buyer places on origin; (c) the **verbal-description-vs-actual-product** finding is the academic warrant for demonstration over description, i.e. a working artefact beats a claim. Caveat that must travel with all of this: **this literature is overwhelmingly about physical consumer products.** The services extension is thin — the searched sources state that *"the impact of COO in service organizations has been relatively lightly researched"* — and no meta-analysis for offshore software services was found. C3.12.

### F/22 — Dunford's Positioning Components and 10-Step Process `[ASSERTED — primary practitioner]`

**Five components** (Dunford's own list, verbatim from her Quickstart guide): *"1. Competitive Alternatives 2. Differentiated 'Features' or 'Capabilities' 3. Value for customers 4. Target Customer Segmentation 5. Market Category."*

**Ten steps** (as reported in the secondary literature; the book itself was not read):
1. Understand the customers who love your product.
2. Form a positioning team.
3. Align positioning vocabulary and let go of positioning baggage.
4. **List your true competitive alternatives.**
5. Isolate your unique attributes or features.
6. Map the attributes to value "themes."
7. Determine who cares a lot.
8. **Find a market frame of reference that puts your strengths at the centre.**
9. Layer on a trend.
10. Capture your positioning.

Two steps carry the whole method for this pillar's purposes. Step 4 is the reference-price selection (P3.1). Step 8 is the competitive-frame selection (F/23). Note the ordering: **alternatives are enumerated before the category is chosen.** The category is an output of the analysis, not an input — which is the exact opposite of how B1/B3 proceed, where the category label is chosen first and the alternatives are never enumerated at all. That inversion is C3.2.

**Runnable canvas** — see §6.1.

### F/23 — Competitive-Frame Selection Method `[DERIVED — assembled from Dunford steps 4 and 8, Pillar 1 F5/EVE, and Pillar 1 P1.6]`

The brief asked for a method for choosing who you are compared against, on the grounds that the reference set determines the price expectation. No source publishes such a method as a procedure; Dunford supplies the two ends and the EVE literature supplies the arithmetic. This assembles them. **Labelled derived.**

1. **Enumerate real alternatives, not competitors.** The question is Dunford's: *"What would a customer do if your offering didn't exist?"* Include the alternatives Pillar 1 F5 and Pillar 2 T2.1 already name: do nothing; a coping spreadsheet; hire a person; buy incumbent SaaS; a local agency; an offshore freelancer; an internal build. Restrict to alternatives *observed in real deals* — Dunford is explicit that theoretical competitors do not count.
2. **Price each alternative.** Every entry gets an annualised number. This is Pillar 1 F5 step 1 (`RV`) run once per candidate frame rather than once per deal.
3. **Compute the frame's reference price band** and locate your intended fee inside it. Use Pillar 1 P1.6: a fee at the *bottom* of a frame's band triggers quality doubt; at the *top*, monetary-sacrifice concern.
4. **Score each candidate frame** on the four criteria in F3.2 (§6.3): reference-price height, credibility-requirement, decision-chain length induced, and defensibility of your unique attributes inside that frame.
5. **Select the frame in which your genuine unique attributes are the frame's own evaluation criteria** — Dunford step 8, *"puts your strengths at the centre."* A frame where your strengths are peripheral is a frame you lose in even when you win the argument.
6. **Test the frame against the credibility budget.** A frame with a high reference price also carries a high credibility requirement (F3.3). If your available credibility substitutes cannot cover the requirement, the frame is aspirational, not selected. **This step is the one the internal corpus omits entirely and it is where B1 is most exposed** — "Operations Transformation Partner" is a frame whose reference set is management consultancies and ERP integrators, i.e. the highest reference price *and* the highest credibility requirement available.
7. **Record the frame, and record what would falsify it.** Repositioning is costly (P3.13/§7.6), so the frame should be chosen with a stated review interval — Baker's published interval is every three to five years, back-tested every three to four.

### F/24 — The Credibility-Substitute Catalogue `[structure DERIVED; individual items sourced where possible]`
Full catalogue with cost/trust ranking in **§6.2**. The framework part is the two-axis classification:
- **Axis 1 — Bond vs Burn** (P3.11). A *bond* costs you only if you fail (guarantee, penalty clause, payment-on-acceptance, fixed price). A *burn* costs you regardless (original research, open-source library, published methodology, paid certification, local entity).
- **Axis 2 — Imitability.** Per Spence's axiom, a substitute generates trust in proportion to how much *more* it would cost a bad firm to produce. Testimonials, "we're passionate" copy and stock-photo team pages are near-zero-cost for everyone and therefore near-zero-information.
The catalogue's ranking metric is `Trust generated ÷ Cost to produce`, with the explicit note that a substitute scoring high on that ratio *because it is cheap* is exactly the substitute Spence says will not work. The ratio is a budgeting instrument, not a selection criterion. See F3.4.

### F/25 — Morgan's Five Axes of Specialization and Six-Step Decision Process `[ASSERTED — primary practitioner, fetched]`

**Five types** (Morgan's own taxonomy, with his definitions):
| Type | Definition / example | His stated risk |
|---|---|---|
| **Pure vertical** | *"You do your thing, whatever that is, exclusively for a specific market vertical."* e.g. distance-learning program design for higher education | Susceptible to sector-specific downturns or legislation |
| **Audience** | Businesses sharing something important *across* verticals — non-profits, venture-funded startups, women-owned businesses. Have identifiable *"watering holes"* | (Shares vertical's reach advantages) |
| **Pure horizontal** | *"a focus on a business problem, optimization, or transformation."* e.g. "I help businesses save money by improving their inventory management" | *"more difficult business development task"* with unpredictable sales cycles |
| **Platform** | Around a tool or framework — AWS, React, EOS, Salesforce. *"the low price of entry to a platform specialization is subsidized by some hidden risks"* | *"you don't control the platform"*; ecosystems commoditize within ~7 years |
| **Service** | Narrowing a single service offering rather than the whole business; useful for testing, or as a *"beachhead"* for a pivot | (Lowest commitment, lowest signal) |

His stated non-hierarchy: *"No way of specializing is universally best. They all have pros and cons."* And the reach/interest trade-off: vertical and audience are *"easier to proactively find and reach prospects"* via watering holes; horizontal and platform are *"easier to imagine… will remain interesting… over the long haul"* and *"may be more compatible with solo consultant business models."*

**Six-step decision process:**
1. **Assess current situation** — inventory past experience, interests, entrepreneurial theses on a spreadsheet scored on **Access, Credibility, Impact, Profitability, Interest**.
2. **Assess risk profile** — three components: emotional comfort with uncertainty, financial loss capacity, volatility composure.
3. **Create shortlist** — his published formulas: `Aggregate Score = Impact + Profit + Interest`; `Risk Score = 7 − Access − Credibility`.
4. **Apply guardrails** — market size, competitor presence, accessibility, external threats.
5. **Choose validation depth** — blind pivot (post-hoc validation) / guardrail-and-go (proxy validation) / deep market research (interviews) / live market test (direct feedback on "knowledge gifts").
6. **Implementation** — declare focus, build marketing platform, cultivate ecosystem support.

**His published guardrail numbers** (§7.2): vertical of **1,000–10,000 prospects** optimal; **10–100 competitors** signals healthy demand. Note that step 3's `Risk Score = 7 − Access − Credibility` is the only place in the entire positioning canon I found where **credibility is an explicit input to the specialization choice** rather than a downstream marketing problem. That makes F/25 the framework best matched to Axon Enjin's actual constraint, and it is a better-fitting instrument than F/22 for a services firm with no logos.

### F/26 — The Compliance-Anchored Category `[DERIVED from Pillar 2 §7.5 + P3.7 + Tidemark]`
A positioning pattern rather than a published framework. Steps: (1) select a vertical whose loss is established by statute; (2) make the statute, not the firm, the source of the value claim; (3) supply the *delivery*-credibility separately, since the statute does not vouch for your build capability; (4) expect the Paper Process to *lengthen* even as the value argument shortens — regulated buyers add security review, DPA and data-residency steps, which is Pillar 2's `P_paper` rising while `S_approvals` falls. Tidemark's corroborating statement: *"When mistakes are expensive — fines, safety incidents, license loss — buyers accept premium pricing."* The internal corpus's B5 data-residency note is the concrete instance of the (4) cost.

### F/27 — Value-Mode Migration as a Positioning Objective `[DERIVED — this is Pillar 2's unchased thread]`
Pillar 2 F/17 records Rackham & DeVincentis' three value modes (intrinsic / extrinsic / strategic) and the source's own caution that *"today's Intrinsic Value Customer may soon be motivated primarily by extrinsic or strategic considerations"* — **value mode is a state, not a trait.** Read as a positioning taxonomy, the three modes describe what the seller must *be*:
| Buyer mode | Where value sits | What the seller must be | Consequence for Axon Enjin |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Intrinsic** | In the product | An efficient, cheap, convenient transactor | This is the "software house" frame. Consultative selling *destroys* margin here. Rate cards are expected. Pillar 1 C11's procurement problem lives here. |
| **Extrinsic** | In the advice and the wrapper | A configurer, integrator, adviser | This is the frame B1 is reaching for. Value pricing becomes available. |
| **Strategic** | In the mutual capability relationship | A partner with a joint roadmap | Requires a track record the firm does not have. |
The positioning claim, derived: **a firm cannot choose its buyers' value mode, but it can choose a frame that makes one mode the natural reading of the purchase.** A buyer who arrives in intrinsic mode ("get me three quotes for an inventory app") can in principle be moved to extrinsic mode, and the instrument for the move is the diagnostic sequence (Pillar 5) plus the frame (F/23). This is the mechanism that makes B1 *coherent* — B1 is, precisely, an attempt to force an extrinsic-mode reading. **No source found measures whether mode migration succeeds, at what rate, or how often the attempt costs the deal.** C3.6.

### F/28 — Play Bigger's Category Design `[ASSERTED — practitioner, with a widely-repeated statistic whose methodology is weak]`
The claim: a **Category King** *"creates, defines and ultimately dominates a category… It defines the problem the market should care about, positions itself as the obvious solution and conditions customers, investors and the media to think inside its own frame."* Named examples: Salesforce (CRM), AWS (public cloud). Headline statistic: category kings capture **76% of the market capitalisation** of their category, from a multi-year analysis of investment and market-cap data for venture-backed companies funded since 2000, highlighted in *Harvard Business Review* Jan–Feb 2016. Their stated market-cap methodology: public market cap from third parties (Bloomberg, Google Finance); or acquisition purchase price; or most recent post-money private valuation.
**Reliability note, stated plainly.** The 76% figure is frequently described as *"peer reviewed data published in the Harvard Business Review"* — HBR is not a peer-reviewed journal, and the analysis is the authors' own advisory-firm research. The population is **venture-backed technology companies**, selected on outcomes, which is a textbook survivorship-selected sample: categories are identified partly by the fact that someone came to dominate them. Whether the finding transfers to a services firm in a mid-market operational-software niche is entirely untested. Treat as practitioner assertion with a number attached, not as evidence. C3.13.

### F/29 — Ries & Trout's Positioning Laws `[ASSERTED — foundational practitioner, no controlled test found]`
- **Law of Leadership:** *"It's better to be first than it is to be better."* The first brand into a category is the one permanently associated with it; *"it's much easier to get into the mind first than to try to convince someone you have a better product than the one that did get there first."*
- **Law of the Category:** if you cannot be first in a category, create a category you can be first in. *"Customers only have so much mental shelf space, so it's simpler to stand out when you completely control a category."*
The mental-shelf-space premise is, interestingly, the **same cognitive premise Ehrenberg-Bass builds on** — memory constraint — from which the two schools derive opposite prescriptions (own a narrow slot vs. be broadly recallable). That shared premise with divergent conclusions is the cleanest statement of C3.3.

### F/30 — Enns' Expertise-Positioning Argument `[ASSERTED — practitioner; accessed via summaries, not the primary text]`
*The Win Without Pitching Manifesto* is structured as **twelve proclamations** — declarations of practice rather than advice. The core claim relevant here, as reported: *"It is expertise and expertise alone which will give you a position of power over your client."* The stated mechanism is that specialization converts the vendor–buyer relationship from supplicant to practitioner, which is what makes the refusals in Pillar 1's F/3 (no free pitching, no early pricing, invoke policies) sustainable. **The positioning argument and the pricing rules in Pillar 1 are the same argument**: Pillar 1's T15 ("invoke a policy rather than a preference") is only credible from a firm the buyer reads as an expert practice. A generalist vendor invoking a policy reads as difficult; a specialist invoking a policy reads as professional. That linkage is derived, not stated by Enns. **Honesty note: Pillar 1 flagged that its Enns/Baker/Weiss extraction came through secondary summaries. I did not reach the primary text of the Manifesto either.** See the session review.

### F/31 — Ehrenberg-Bass Distinctive Assets & Category Entry Points (the counter-framework) `[EVIDENCED]`
The operational content of P3.10, stated as a framework so it can be run against F/22 and F/28:
1. **Build distinctive assets, not differentiating claims** — name, logo, colours, house style, slogan, characters. The test is *recognition and correct attribution*, not *preference*.
2. **Enumerate category entry points** — the buying situations in which the buyer's need arises. Their B2B examples: *"contract renewal," "compliance audits," "IT infrastructure upgrades."*
3. **Link the brand in memory to as many CEPs as possible** — breadth of memory linkage, not depth of a single association.
4. **Optimise for penetration** — *"B2B brands grow by getting more customers, and therefore increasing brand penetration is the key sales KPI."*
5. **Accept that your competitors are the big brands** — *"you will typically gain and lose more customers to them than smaller brands."*
6. **Stop worrying about rejection** — *"Brand rejection is low and rarely a barrier to growth."*
Note step 2 is a **direct competitor to Dunford's step 8**, and note also that a CEP list is functionally close to Pillar 2's F/16 trigger-signal categories and F/12's Critical Event — three literatures converging on "buying situations" as the unit of analysis. That convergence is the strongest cross-framework signal found in this pillar and it is a P4 handoff.

## 4. Hacks — asymmetric, low-effort, high-leverage

- **H3.1 — The competitive-alternatives list is a one-hour artefact and it prices your whole offer.** F/23 steps 1–3 require no research budget: the alternatives are a hired role's salary, an incumbent SaaS price list, a freelancer marketplace rate, and zero. That single table simultaneously produces the positioning frame (Dunford step 4) and Pillar 1's `RV` (EVE step 1). Two pillars' inputs from one hour of work.
- **H3.2 — The store-locator and careers page you already scrape (Pillar 2 H2.1/H2.2) are also your frame evidence.** The same public data that scores the prospect supplies the reference price you will compare yourself against in the room.
- **H3.3 — Publish the teardown of a public artefact, not a case study you don't have.** A dated, specific, numbers-bearing teardown of a *publicly observable* operational failure in the target vertical requires no client permission, no logo, and no track record — and it is a costly signal in Spence's sense because being wrong is publicly embarrassing. It is the only high-imitability-cost credibility substitute available on day one.
- **H3.4 — One original micro-survey beats ten testimonials.** N=40 in a named vertical, published with method and figures, is a burn signal that a competitor must actually replicate. Testimonials are free for everyone and therefore carry near-zero information (P3.5).
- **H3.5 — The named process is free and it converts invisible repetition into a visible cue.** B4's reuse claim is unverifiable by a buyer. A named, documented, four-stage method with defined artefacts per stage *is* verifiable at proposal time.
- **H3.6 — A bond is the cheapest high-cost signal a cash-poor firm has.** Payment on acceptance, a defined-scope fixed price, a stated revision allowance, or source-code custody all cost zero unless you fail. Guarded by T3.8.
- **H3.7 — Local cues are individually trivial and jointly load-bearing.** B5's .com.au domain and AU phone number are low-cost items whose value is not their own persuasiveness but their **cue count** — the multiple-cue dilution effect (T3.5). Cheap items that reduce the weight on an expensive liability.
- **H3.8 — Category entry points are free positioning insurance.** Whatever happens to the category-design bet (C3.3), listing the five buying situations in which your prospect's need surfaces, and making sure you are the recalled name in each, costs nothing and is compatible with either school.
- **H3.9 — Service-line specialization is a reversible bet; vertical specialization is not.** Morgan's own framing of service specialization as a *"beachhead"* makes it the cheapest available option value while the plan's Open Question remains unanswered.
- **H3.10 — Let the statute do the arguing.** In a compliance vertical the most persuasive sentence in the pitch is a quotation from legislation, which costs nothing to acquire and cannot be attributed to vendor self-interest.

## 5. Terms — glossary

| Term | One-line definition |
|---|---|
| **Positioning** | Morgan: *"the process of seeking and earning a specific, desired location in a market."* A market fact about what you are compared against — not a statement you make. |
| **Specialization** | Morgan: *"the decision you make about how to focus your business."* A unilateral act of the firm; the input to positioning. |
| **Messaging** | The communication of a position. Dunford: *"Positioning is not equivalent to messaging. It isn't a tagline."* |
| **Competitive alternative** | What the buyer would do if you did not exist — including nothing. Identical in content to Pillar 1's Reference Value. |
| **Market frame of reference / market category** | The comparison set that sets buyer assumptions about features, buyer, competitors and price. |
| **Reference-price effect of frame choice** | The change in the buyer's expected price caused purely by changing the comparison set, at constant offer. |
| **Category design** | Play Bigger: deliberately defining a new market category and conditioning the market to evaluate inside your frame. |
| **Category king** | The firm that creates, defines and dominates a category; claimed to capture ~76% of category market cap. |
| **Law of Leadership** | Ries & Trout: *"It's better to be first than it is to be better."* |
| **Law of the Category** | If you cannot be first in a category, create a category you can be first in. |
| **Differentiation** | Ehrenberg-Bass: *"features of a product or service that differentiate a brand in the eyes of the customer."* About meaning. |
| **Distinctiveness** | Ehrenberg-Bass: *"making your brand easily identified by customers."* About memory and correct attribution. |
| **Mental availability / salience** | *"the brand being easily thought of in buying situations."* |
| **Physical availability** | *"the brand being easy to find and buy."* |
| **Category entry point (CEP)** | A buying situation that triggers the need — e.g. contract renewal, compliance audit, infrastructure upgrade. The unit of memory linkage in the Ehrenberg-Bass model. |
| **Double Jeopardy** | Small brands have both fewer buyers and slightly lower loyalty; reported to hold in B2B. |
| **Vertical specialization** | Serving one market vertical exclusively. |
| **Horizontal specialization** | Solving one business problem across verticals. |
| **Audience specialization** | Serving businesses sharing a non-industry attribute across verticals. |
| **Platform specialization** | Specializing around a tool or ecosystem; Morgan: ecosystems commoditize in ~7 years and *"you don't control the platform."* |
| **Service specialization** | Narrowing a single offering rather than the firm; usable as a reversible beachhead. |
| **Beachhead** | A narrow initial position chosen for reachability and credibility, intended to be widened or pivoted from. |
| **Watering hole** | A place a specialized audience predictably congregates; the reach advantage of vertical/audience specialization. |
| **Resource partitioning** | Carroll: generalist concentration enlarges the peripheral resource space and thereby improves specialist survival. |
| **Liability of newness** | Stinchcombe 1965: new organizations fail at higher rates; mechanism is that participants are strangers and trust takes time. |
| **Liability of smallness** | The separable size-based component of the same hazard; usually co-occurs with newness. |
| **Liability of adolescence** | Brüderl & Schüssler's inverted-U alternative: hazard peaks roughly 1–15 years post-founding after an initial endowment-funded honeymoon. |
| **Organizational legitimacy** | The resource that lets a new venture acquire other resources; enhanced by conformance, selection, manipulation and creation strategies. |
| **Signal (economic)** | An observable, costly action taken to convey unobservable quality. |
| **Spence's axiom** | A signal is informative only if its cost is negatively correlated with quality — expensive enough that low-quality imitation is prohibitive. |
| **Separating equilibrium** | The outcome in which high- and low-quality parties self-sort because the signal is differentially costly. |
| **Bond signal** | A signal whose cost is incurred only on non-performance — guarantee, warranty, penalty, payment-on-acceptance. |
| **Burn signal** | A signal whose cost is sunk regardless of outcome — original research, open source, certification, local entity. |
| **Warrantor credibility** | Boulding & Kirmani: the condition under which a bond is believed at all. |
| **Credibility substitute** | Any artefact or structure that stands in for a track record you do not have. |
| **Proof-of-work artefact** | A demonstrable output — teardown, working prototype, benchmark, open-source library — as distinct from a claim about outputs. |
| **Borrowed credibility / trust transfer** | Legitimacy acquired by verifiable association with an already-legitimate party. |
| **Country-of-origin (COO) effect** | The influence of a product's or service's origin on evaluation; measured mean effect .30 on quality, .19 on purchase intention. |
| **Single-cue vs multiple-cue** | COO effects are larger when origin is the only available cue; additional cues dilute it. |
| **Offshore trust discount** | The corpus's informal name for the COO penalty applied to an emerging-market services vendor. |
| **Value mode (intrinsic / extrinsic / strategic)** | Rackham & DeVincentis: where the buyer locates value. A *state*, not a trait — hence movable by positioning. |
| **Point of view / manifesto positioning** | Positioning by publishing a stated, contestable position on how the work should be done; Enns' twelve proclamations are the form of record. |
| **Repositioning risk** | The cost and hazard of changing an established position — customer alienation, message inconsistency, sunk brand investment. |
| **T-shaped expertise** | Baker: deep subject-matter depth plus broad light learning. |

## 6. Formulas, canvases and instruments

> **Statement required by the brief:** most of this pillar has **no legitimate formalism.** Positioning theory is not quantified anywhere I could find. Specifically, there is **no published formula** for: a specialization premium; the price effect of a category change; the trust value of a credibility substitute; the size of the offshore trust discount in services; or the survival benefit of a chosen position. I have not invented formulas to fill those slots. What follows is: two **worksheets** (§6.1, §6.2), which is the honest form for this material; four **derived scoring instruments** (§6.3–§6.6) clearly labelled as priors; and one **genuine published formula pair** (Morgan's, §6.7). Where a number appears without a source, it is a prior of mine, and it says so.

1. **The highest-ratio items (1, 2, 3, 4, 18) all require a completed engagement.** The catalogue is therefore not a substitute for the plan's Open Question — it is a bootstrapping ladder whose first rung is one delivered piece of work. The sponsored-build campaign (Pillar 4) is, in this reading, a **purchase of catalogue items 1–4** at the cost computed by Pillar 1's F16 and Pillar 2's F2.1.
2. **Items 8, 9, 11, 19 are the only high-imitability-cost substitutes available with zero client history.** They are all *published-artefact* substitutes, and they are all slow. That is the actual answer to "what does a firm with no logos do first," and it is an uncomfortable one because it is a time cost, not a cash cost.
3. **The bonds (5, 6, 7, 18) are the only substitutes that are cheap now and expensive only if you are bad** — which is precisely Spence's separating condition. This is the most theoretically well-founded item in the entire pillar, and it is the least discussed in the internal corpus.

### 6.3 — F3.1: Frame Scoring Instrument `[DERIVED — my priors]`

```
FrameScore = w_r·R + w_a·A − w_c·C − w_d·D

R = reference-price height   (0–4: 0 = free/DIY, 4 = institutional consultancy)
A = attribute centrality    (0–4: are OUR unique attributes this frame's own
                             evaluation criteria?)
C = credibility requirement (0–4: §6.4)
D = decision-chain penalty  (0–4: scaled from expected DCL, §6.5)
Priors: w_r = 1.0, w_a = 1.5, w_c = 1.5, w_d = 1.0
```
Attribute centrality and credibility requirement are weighted heaviest because they are the two the internal corpus never scores. **The weights are conventions, not fitted coefficients — the same standing limitation Pillar 2 §6.5 declares.**

**Worked (illustrative, from §6.1's table):**
- Frame A (software house): `R=1, A=0, C=2, D=2` → `1.0 + 0 − 3.0 − 2.0 = ` **−4.0**
- Frame B (Operations Transformation Partner): `R=4, A=2, C=4, D=3` → `4.0 + 3.0 − 6.0 − 3.0 = ` **−2.0**
- Frame C (multi-branch reconciliation specialist): `R=3, A=4, C=3, D=1` → `3.0 + 6.0 − 4.5 − 1.0 = ` **+3.5**

The instrument's only real content is that it forces `C` and `D` to be scored at all. Change the weights and the ranking of A and B flips; C wins under any weighting where `w_a ≥ w_r`. That sensitivity is a property of the instrument, not a finding.

### 6.4 — F3.2/F3.3: Credibility Requirement and Coverage `[DERIVED]`

```
CredReq(frame) = max over the frame's incumbents of the institutional
                 assurance a buyer expects from them          (0–4 ordinal)
   0 = none (freelancer marketplace)
   1 = a portfolio
   2 = named references in the buyer's country
   3 = domain-specific references plus a documented process
   4 = institutional assurance: audited entity, insurance, certification,
       multi-year references

CredHeld  = the highest level fully covered by substitutes actually in hand
            (§6.2). A level is "covered" only if every requirement at and
            below it is met.

Coverage  = CredHeld − CredReq
   Coverage ≥ 0  → the frame is currently occupiable
   Coverage < 0  → the frame is aspirational; the gap is the work
```

**Signal-cost threshold — the one place a real formalism exists.** Spence's axiom gives a genuine inequality rather than an invented formula. Let `c_H` be the cost to a genuinely capable firm of producing substitute `s`, and `c_L` the cost to an incapable firm. Let `V` be the value of being believed.

```
Signal s is informative  ⟺  c_L > V > c_H
```

Read that carefully, because it is the whole theory of credibility substitutes in one line: the substitute must be affordable to you *and* unaffordable to a firm that would fail. **Testimonials, taglines and brand polish violate the left inequality** (`c_L` is trivially low), which is why they generate no trust regardless of execution quality. **Certifications and multi-year references may violate the right inequality** for a young firm (`c_H > V` today). The substitutes that satisfy both are exactly items 5–11 and 18 in §6.2: bonds, and published falsifiable artefacts. *This is a restatement of Spence's condition, not a new formula; it is quantified only ordinally because no source assigns money values to `c_L` or `c_H` for services credibility signals.*

### 6.5 — F3.4: Category-Induced Decision-Chain Length `[DERIVED — discharges Pillar 2's C2.4 handoff]`

Pillar 2's F2.7 is `DCL = S_approvals + P_paper`, `Cycle_est = a + b·DCL` with priors `a ≈ 25`, `b ≈ 20`. The addition here is that both terms are functions of the frame:

```
DCL(frame) = S_approvals(frame) + P_paper(frame)

ΔCycle = b · [ DCL(frame_2) − DCL(frame_1) ]
```

**Worked, illustrative, Archetype R:** Frame C (`DCL = 2`) → `Cycle = 25 + 40 = 65` days. Frame B (`DCL = 6`) → `Cycle = 25 + 120 = 145` days. `ΔCycle = 20 × 4 = 80 days` **attributable to the frame label alone at constant offer and constant buyer.** Feeding through Pillar 2's F2.8: at `L_total = PHP 777,600`, `PCR` moves from PHP 388,800/link (Frame C) to PHP 129,600/link (Frame B) — a **3× deterioration in the ratio Pillar 2 P2.2 says is the actual selection variable**, caused by nothing but the category claimed.

**This is the single most decision-relevant derived result in the pillar, and it rests entirely on priors.** `S_approvals(frame)` and `P_paper(frame)` are my estimates. **No source found measures decision-chain length as a function of vendor self-categorisation.** C3.7.

### 6.6 — F3.5: Anchor Survivability under a Credibility Deficit `[DERIVED — discharges Pillar 1's C8 handoff]`

Pillar 1 C8 asked whether high anchoring survives in a low-trust, no-track-record, cross-border sale, and recorded that **no source tests anchoring under a credibility deficit.** I searched for one and did not find one either. What can be assembled is a *conditional* statement, not a measurement:

```
An anchor at the top of a frame's reference band is survivable only if
   Coverage(frame) ≥ 0                                     (§6.4)
   AND the buyer's origin-cue weight has been diluted       (T3.5)
   AND at least one bond signal is attached                 (§6.2 items 5–7, 18)
```

Reasoning, assembled from three separate literatures and **not tested jointly by any of them**: (a) Pillar 1 P1.6 — high prices trigger monetary-sacrifice concern, which is a *risk* concern; (b) the COO meta-analysis — origin depresses quality perception with effect .30, and quality perception is the input to Monroe's price → quality → value chain, so the origin penalty and the high-price sacrifice concern **compound rather than offset**; (c) Boulding & Kirmani — a bond raises quality perception specifically *for sellers lacking established reputation*, which is the only mechanism found that pushes in the opposite direction. The derived conclusion is that a high anchor without a bond is the least survivable combination available, and a high anchor *with* a bond is the mechanism by which the anchor might hold. **This is an argument, not a finding.** C3.5.

Scored per candidate specialization on his five-column spreadsheet (Access, Credibility, Impact, Profitability, Interest). The scale is not stated in the source I fetched; the `7 −` constant implies a 1–3 or 0–3 per-item scale.

### 6.8 — Formulas that do not exist and were not invented

Stated explicitly, per the brief:
- **Specialization premium.** No published formula or measured coefficient. Baker asserts a premium via inelastic demand; Hinge reports firm-level growth/profit correlations for specialists but **publishes no fee premium**. A `Price_specialist = Price_generalist × (1 + σ)` form would be pure invention and `σ` has no source. Not supplied.

## 8. Contradictions — stated as open questions, deliberately unresolved

**C3.1 — The category reframe raises the reference price and the credibility requirement together, and the corpus prices only the first.**
B1/B2 want a frame whose reference set is expensive (management consultancy, transformation programmes) so that the fee reads as cheap. §6.1's worked comparison and §6.4 show that the same move raises `CredReq` to its maximum — the frame's incumbents are institutions offering audited entities, insurance and multi-year references — and §6.5 shows it lengthens `DCL`, degrading Pillar 2's `PCR` by ~3×. Frame C (narrow, specific, operational) scores better on `A`, `C` and `D` while capturing less of `R`. *Open question: is the correct move to claim the expensive frame and buy the credibility, or to claim a narrow frame whose reference price is a salary and whose credibility requirement the firm can actually cover?* No source adjudicates. The internal corpus contains **both** answers — `chatgpt2.md` argues the first (B1), `claude-response2.md` argues the second (B4, "pick one vertical") — and does not notice they conflict.

**C3.2 — B3 is a messaging change presented as a positioning change.**
Under Dunford (P3.1) and Morgan (P3.4), a position is *earned* and is constituted by the comparison set. B3 changes a sentence. Dunford's own method places the category label at step 10, *after* alternatives are enumerated at step 4 and the frame is selected at step 8; B1/B3 assert the label first and never enumerate alternatives at all. *Open question: does changing the self-description change the buyer's comparison set — and if so, at what rate and with what failure mode?* No source found tests whether vendor self-categorisation moves buyer reference sets. Morgan's position implies it does not, absent slow reputational accumulation.

**C3.3 — Category design versus mental availability. The pillar's deepest conflict, and it is not resolvable from the sources.**
Ries & Trout, Play Bigger, Dunford, Baker, Enns, Morgan and Stark all hold that a narrow, differentiated, owned position is the mechanism of pricing power. Ehrenberg-Bass hold, on a large replicated empirical programme extending into B2B, that *"customers don't care much if your brand is different"* and that growth comes from broad mental availability and penetration. Note the two schools **share a premise** — memory is constrained — and derive opposite prescriptions from it. Note also three things that complicate a clean win for either side: (i) Ehrenberg-Bass's programme is about *brand growth in established categories with repeat purchase*, and a bespoke six-figure operational build is a rare, high-consideration, single-purchase decision — arguably outside the model's tested domain, though the institute makes no such concession; (ii) their prescription "increase penetration" is not available to a four-person studio with a delivery-capacity ceiling, so even if true it may be inapplicable rather than wrong; (iii) their own claim that *"B2B buyers compare the two most popular options"* is, read against Dunford, an argument that **being in the considered set at all** is the binding constraint — which is a *positioning* argument in Dunford's vocabulary. *Open question, left fully open: for a capacity-constrained services firm, is the binding constraint being differentiated or being remembered — and does the answer differ before and after the first ten engagements?* The academic dissent inside marketing science (Thomaz; and Ehrenberg's own 1993 *Nature* piece) means this is contested **within** the empirical camp too.

**C3.4 — Frame substitution as the answer to the price-quality inference is untested.**
P3.2 and Pillar 1 C5. The claim that changing the comparison set neutralises the low-price quality signal is a mechanism assembled from Dunford plus Rao & Monroe; **no source tests it.** Worse, §6.6's assembly suggests the origin penalty (.30 on quality perception) and the low-price quality inference operate on the *same* dependent variable and therefore compound. *Open question: does an offshore studio pricing at B6's "50–65% of local agency pricing" suffer one quality penalty or two — and does a frame change remove either?*

**C3.5 — High anchoring under a credibility deficit remains untested, as Pillar 1 said.**
I searched specifically for it and found nothing. §6.6 supplies a *conditional* argument (anchor high only with coverage, cue dilution and a bond) built from three literatures that have never been run jointly. *Open question, inherited and not discharged: is Enns' "anchor high, lead with the premium option" robust when the seller's quality is unobservable and the origin cue is negative?* The one directional finding available cuts **for** the bond, not for the anchor: Boulding & Kirmani's warranty effects are strongest precisely for sellers lacking established reputation.

**C3.6 — Value-mode migration is asserted, never measured.**
F/27 makes Pillar 2's unchased thread explicit: mode is a state, so positioning can move it. But nothing in Rackham & DeVincentis or anywhere else found measures how often an intrinsic-mode buyer can be moved to extrinsic mode, at what cost, or how often the attempt loses the deal. *Open question: is mode migration a real seller capability or a post-hoc description of buyers who were already extrinsic?* Note the stakes: if migration fails, B1's whole purpose fails, because a buyer who stays in intrinsic mode will demand exactly the rate card Pillar 1 C11 says the firm cannot give.

**C3.7 — The category→decision-chain link is the pillar's most decision-relevant result and it rests entirely on my priors.**
§6.5 produces an 80-day cycle difference and a 3× `PCR` swing from the category label alone. Both numbers come from `S_approvals(frame)` and `P_paper(frame)` estimates I made. **No source measures decision-chain length as a function of vendor self-description.** *Open question: does calling yourself an operations partner rather than a software house actually keep IT and procurement out of the deal, or does the *contract value* determine that regardless of the label?* The second possibility would void the mechanism entirely, and it is at least as plausible.

**C3.8 — The credibility catalogue's best items require the thing the firm lacks.**
§6.2's five highest-ratio substitutes (reference calls, referrals, working demonstrations on buyer data, detailed case studies, paid pilots) all presuppose at least one completed engagement. The remaining high-imitability-cost items (teardowns, original research, published POV, sustained thought leadership) are all *slow*. *Open question: for a firm at zero, is the correct expenditure cash (buy engagements via sponsored builds — Pillar 4) or time (publish falsifiable artefacts until inbound arrives)?* The corpus's campaign docs assume the first without costing the second. Pillar 1's F16 and Pillar 2's F2.1 price the first side of that trade only.

**C3.9 — Compliance positioning solves the value-credibility problem and may worsen the delivery-credibility problem.**
P3.7 and F/26. The statute establishes the loss without your cooperation — genuinely powerful, and the one place a young firm can make an unarguable value claim. But regulated buyers are exactly the buyers who add security review, DPA, data residency and insurance requirements, i.e. `CredReq` at level 4 and `P_paper` rising. *Open question: does the compliance vertical's third-party-established value outweigh its third-party-imposed credibility bar for a firm with no certifications?* Pillar 2 called compliance a positioning asset. This pillar finds it is an asset on one axis and a liability on the other, and no source nets them.

**C3.10 — The academic hazard literature does not agree on whether firm youth is the hazard.**
Stinchcombe's liability of newness versus Brüderl & Schüssler's liability of adolescence (inverted-U, peak 1–15 years) versus recent re-confirmations finding *higher* early hazard than previously measured. *Open question left open: is a two-year-old studio in its most dangerous period, or in an endowment-funded honeymoon before the dangerous period?* The two answers imply opposite urgencies about acquiring durable positions. Note also that Stinchcombe's trust mechanism is *internal* (participants are strangers to each other); the extension to *buyer* trust is an inference made by this document and by the legitimacy literature, not by Stinchcombe.

**C3.11 — Resource partitioning supports niching, but its precondition is unverified here.**
Carroll's theory predicts specialist proliferation *conditional on* a concentrated generalist population and a heterogeneous periphery. It is the only academic support for niching found. *Open question: is AU/PH mid-market operational software a concentrated generalist market with an unserved heterogeneous periphery, or a fragmented market with no generalist centre at all?* If the latter, the theory does not apply and the niching argument reverts to pure consultant assertion. Pillar 2 could not size these populations either.

**C3.12 — The offshore trust discount is measured, but on the wrong objects.**
The .30/.19 effect sizes are strong, primary and replicated — for **consumer products**. The reviews themselves say COO in services is *"relatively lightly researched."* Axon Enjin's case is services, B2B, high-consideration, relational, and cross-border. *Open question: do the single-cue dilution and demonstration-over-description findings — the two most actionable results in the pillar — transfer to a B2B services purchase?* I am applying them because they are the best available mechanism, and flagging that the transfer is unvalidated.

**C3.13 — The 76% category-king statistic will not bear the weight put on it.**
Survivorship-selected sample of venture-backed technology companies; categories partly identified by the existence of a dominant firm; published in HBR (not peer-reviewed) by the advisory firm that sells the methodology; frequently mis-described in the secondary literature as *"peer reviewed."* *Open question: is there any evidence for category design outside venture-backed technology, and specifically any for services firms?* I found none.

**C3.14 — Hinge's own specialization numbers are internally inconsistent, and the whole claim is correlational.**
The same research programme is reported as *"up to 30% more profitable"* and *"almost 2X as profitable"*, and the accessed page states *"most"* high-growth firms self-identify as specialists without publishing the percentage. More importantly the design is a self-report survey with the causal arrow unestablished: **fast-growing firms may specialize because they grew, not grow because they specialized.** And the study publishes no **fee premium** at all, which is the specific claim Baker makes and which B4 depends on. *Open question: is there any measurement anywhere of specialists' fees against generalists' fees for comparable work?* Not found at any tier. This is the second consecutive pillar unable to find willingness-to-pay data.

**C3.15 — The corpus's own two positioning recommendations are different specializations, not one.**
B1 ("Operations Transformation Partner") is a **horizontal** specialization in Morgan's taxonomy — a business problem across verticals. B4 ("pick one vertical", nine dental groups) is a **vertical** specialization. Morgan's taxonomy makes them alternatives with opposite risk profiles: vertical gives reach via watering holes and exposes you to sector shocks; horizontal is *"a more difficult business development task"* with unpredictable sales cycles. B4's reuse and estimation argument (the margin argument) **requires the vertical**; B1's reference-price argument (the pricing argument) **is served by the horizontal**. *Open question: can both be held, e.g. horizontal category label on a vertical delivery base — and if so, which one does the buyer actually use as the comparison set?* Nothing found addresses combined positioning for a firm without an established position, though Baker recommends *tightening* over time by combining vertical and horizontal factors — for firms that already have one.

**C3.16 — Distinctiveness is cheap and the corpus ignores it; differentiation is expensive and the corpus assumes it.**
If Ehrenberg-Bass is even partly right for this buying situation, the highest-return positioning expenditure is being *recalled at the category entry point*, not being *different*. The internal corpus contains no distinctive-asset thinking at all. *Open question, and the cheapest experiment in the pillar: which of the two produces more inbound for a studio at zero — a narrow differentiated claim, or being the recalled name attached to three specific buying situations?*

**C3.17 — The hackathon record is externally-administered proof of the wrong variable.**
It is a genuine costly signal (item 17, §6.2) — externally judged, not self-asserted, which puts it above almost everything else the firm can currently show on the imitability axis. But the unobservable a mid-market buyer needs resolved is **delivery reliability and domain judgement over months**, not build skill under time pressure. *Open question: does competition performance transfer as a credibility cue to an operations buyer, or does it actively reinforce the "clever students" reading that positioning is trying to escape?* No source found on competition records as B2B credibility signals.

**C3.18 — Repositioning cost is asserted by parties who sell repositioning.**
§7.6's cost bands come from agencies selling the service. No base rate for repositioning failure was found at any tier. *Open question: what does it actually cost a small services firm to abandon a stated specialization?* Unanswered — which matters because it determines whether T3.14's service-line beachhead is genuinely the low-regret option I have called it.

## End-to-End Session Review

### What I searched and why

Nineteen web searches and nine page fetches, in six deliberate groups.

1. **Canon extraction** (Dunford, Baker, Morgan, Enns, Ries & Trout, Play Bigger, Stark). Purpose: get each author's *own* framework rather than a paraphrase, since the brief demanded a runnable canvas and the internal corpus had adopted a category label without any method behind it. Two fetches did the real work: **Dunford's own Quickstart guide**, which yielded the price-expectation quotation verbatim and therefore the pillar's central mechanism (P3.1); and **Philip Morgan's specialization guide**, which yielded the only published formulas in the canon and the only framework in which credibility is an *input* to the specialization decision rather than a downstream marketing chore. Morgan's guide was the highest-yield call of the session.
2. **The mandated counter-position** (Ehrenberg-Bass, Byron Sharp, Double Jeopardy in B2B, academic pushback). Purpose: the brief instructed me to take this seriously and not present category design as settled. Two institute fetches gave verbatim definitions and the B2B applicability claims. I then deliberately searched for the *academic criticism of Sharp*, which produced the Thomaz critique and the pointer to Ehrenberg's own 1993 *Nature* article — meaning the counter-position is itself contested from inside marketing science. That turned C3.3 from a two-sided contradiction into a three-sided one, which is the honest shape.
3. **Signaling theory** (Spence 1973; warranty-as-signal; Kirmani & Rao). Purpose: the brief said this is the rigorous route into credibility substitutes, and it is. Spence's axiom — cost negatively correlated with quality — is what let me build §6.2 on a principled axis (imitability cost) rather than on a vibes ranking, and it is what let me state flatly that testimonial pages carry no information. The Boulding & Kirmani finding that warranty effects are strongest **for sellers lacking established reputation** is the single most directly applicable primary finding in the pillar.
4. **Country-of-origin** (meta-analyses; services offshoring). Purpose: the brief said find the actual academic work, not blog summaries. Peterson & Jolibert's .30/.19 with the single-cue and verbal-description moderators is the best quantitative material in the document and it converted two of the internal corpus's intuitions (B5's local signals; demonstration over claims) from hunches into mechanisms with an academic warrant.
5. **Organizational theory** (liability of newness/smallness/adolescence; new-venture legitimacy; resource partitioning). Purpose: the brief named these and they turned out to be the only place where "young firm" and "specialist firm" are treated as measurable conditions rather than marketing problems. The discovery that the newness literature *disagrees with itself* (Brüderl & Schüssler's adolescence hazard) was unplanned and became C3.10.
6. **Empirical checks on specialization and on guarantees** (Hinge High Growth Study; risk-reversal effect sizes; repositioning cost). Purpose: the brief demanded I separate evidenced from asserted. This group is where the separation bit hardest: the Hinge fetch **disconfirmed** what I expected to find, showing the specialization–performance claim is correlational, self-reported, internally inconsistent on the profitability multiple, and silent on fees.

The **founder-age search**, which produced entrepreneurship-and-fundraising literature and one qualitative B2B age-impressions study, but nothing measuring a young-founder penalty in a *purchase* decision. The **repositioning-risk search**, which returned only branding-agency marketing; there is no base rate for repositioning failure at any tier I could reach.

### Handoff notes

**For Pillar 4 — Demand Generation & Offer Design**

- **Three literatures independently converge on "buying situations" as the unit of demand generation, and none of them cites the others.** Ehrenberg-Bass's **category entry points** (F/31 step 2), Winning by Design's **Critical Event** (P2 F/12), and the trigger-signal taxonomy (P2 F/16) are the same construct arriving from brand science, sales methodology and prospecting practice. That triple convergence is the strongest cross-framework signal found in this pillar, and it means a CEP list is simultaneously a memory-structure asset, a discovery framework slot, and a target list. Build it once, use it three ways.
- **The sponsored-build campaign is, in this pillar's vocabulary, a purchase of credibility-substitute items 1–4 from §6.2** (reference call, referral, working demonstration on the buyer's own data, detailed case study with figures) — which are the four highest-ratio items in the catalogue and the only ones that require a completed engagement. That reframing makes Pillar 1's F16 (Free-Work Payback) and Pillar 2's F2.1 (MVDS) the *price* of the credibility, and it makes the campaign's real deliverable the artefacts, not the client. **Design the sponsored engagement backwards from the artefacts you need**: named-reference permission, a checkable number, a written method. A sponsored build that yields no citable figure has bought nothing.
- **C3.8 is your allocation question and it is unresolved.** For a firm at zero, the two routes are cash (buy engagements) and time (publish falsifiable artefacts — teardowns, micro-surveys, a named process, sustained writing). The corpus only costs the first. §6.2 items 8, 9, 11, 19 are the second route and they are the *only* high-imitability-cost substitutes available with zero client history. Someone should cost the time route before the cash route is committed.
- **Spence's axiom is a filter for your entire content plan.** Anything a bad firm could produce as cheaply as you carries no information. That rules out testimonial pages, "passionate team" copy, generic thought-leadership, and unnumbered case studies — and it rules *in* teardowns with figures, original micro-surveys, and published falsifiable benchmarks. This is the sharpest available answer to "what content actually builds trust," and it is a theoretical answer, not an evidenced one (C3.8, and the "could not find" list).
- **Demonstration beats description, with an academic warrant.** Peterson & Jolibert: verbal descriptions produce *larger* origin penalties than an actual product present; single cues produce larger penalties than multiple cues. Every offer artefact should therefore be a working thing plus several independent cues, not a claim. This is the most operationally useful evidenced finding in the pillar and it belongs in your offer design, not just your messaging.
- **Bonds are the cheapest strong signal available now and the corpus never discusses them.** §6.2 items 5, 6, 7, 18 — bounded guarantee, payment on acceptance, source-code custody, paid pilot — cost nothing unless the firm fails, which is exactly Spence's separating condition. The paid-pilot form in particular converges with Pillar 2's JOLT/FOMU finding (56% of no-decision losses are indecision, not status-quo preference) and with the Challenger Skeptic's *"small wins first."* Three independent lines pointing at a small, bonded first offer. Guarded by T3.8: never offer a bond you could not honour.
- **One structural warning inherited and sharpened.** Pillar 2 ended by noting nothing in the program addresses **access** — whether the economic buyer of a 20-site group will take a call from a studio with no logos. This pillar's answer is only partial: §6.2's top two items are referral and reference, both of which *are* access mechanisms, and both of which require a prior engagement. **Access and credibility are the same bootstrapping problem, and the corpus has no first move for it.** That is P4's central problem, not a footnote to it.

**For Pillar 5 — The Sales Conversation**

- **The competitive-alternatives table (§6.1 row 1) is your discovery script's spine and it is already Pillar 1's EVE step 1.** Dunford's question *"What would a customer do if your offering didn't exist?"* is a discovery question, not a strategy exercise. Asking it in the room simultaneously produces the reference price, the frame, and Pillar 2's Conscious-Loss evidence.
- **T3.2 is an objection-handling move with a mechanism, and it belongs in your objection library.** When a buyer says "a freelancer would do this for a fifth of that," the canonical move is not to justify the price but to change the comparison set — to the salaried person, the annual loss, or the incumbent SaaS bill. Note honestly in the library that C3.4 records this as untested, and that the origin penalty and the low-price quality penalty may compound rather than cancel.
- **F/27 (value-mode migration) is the most interesting instrument this pillar produced and it is yours to make real or to kill.** A buyer arriving in intrinsic mode ("send me three quotes") will demand the rate card Pillar 1's C11 says cannot be given; a buyer in extrinsic mode will accept a value conversation. If mode migration is a real seller capability, the diagnostic sequence is the instrument that performs it — and P5 is where that would happen. **C3.6 says nobody has measured whether it works or how often attempting it loses the deal.** If P5 finds nothing either, the honest conclusion is that B1's entire purpose is unsupported.
- **The youth objection has a *structural* answer, not a rhetorical one (T3.19).** The liability-of-newness mechanism is trust built over time between strangers. The available responses are bonds and structures — milestone acceptance, escrow, source-code custody, a short bounded first engagement — not arguments about the founders' ages or the team's talent. Note also C3.17: the hackathon record is externally-administered proof, which makes it a *real* signal, of build skill rather than of delivery reliability, and it may reinforce the "clever students" reading it is meant to escape. Handle deliberately.
- **Pillar 1's C8 is still open after two pillars.** §6.6 gives you the conditional form: a high anchor is survivable only with credibility coverage, cue dilution, and an attached bond. The one directional primary finding available cuts **for the bond, not for the anchor** — Boulding & Kirmani's warranty effects are strongest precisely for sellers lacking reputation. If P5 tests only one thing, test whether a bond makes a high anchor hold.
- **In compliance verticals, separate the two credibility claims explicitly in the conversation (T3.13).** The statute establishes the *value* and needs no vouching. It establishes nothing about whether you can build the thing. Buyers will conflate them and the seller must not.
- **Enns' expertise premise is load-bearing across P1, P3 and P5, and it is unevidenced in all three.** Pillar 1's T15 ("invoke a policy") and F/3's refusals only read as professional from a firm the buyer already accepts as a specialist practice; from a generalist vendor they read as difficult. **The pricing discipline and the positioning are the same bet.** If P5 builds an objection library on the assumption that policies can be invoked, it inherits that bet, and neither pillar has yet reached Enns' primary text to test what he actually claims.
